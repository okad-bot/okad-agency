RaaS for UGC: Pay for Conversions, Not Videos

The traditional UGC model charges per video and hopes for results. RaaS UGC ties payment to performance — CPA, ROAS, win rate. Here is how to structure it, with data from 7 campaigns.

Guide9 min readby the OKAD team

The standard UGC transaction looks like this: a brand pays an agency or creator $200–$400 per video, receives a batch of 10–20 assets, launches them as ads, and discovers that 15–25% perform above the CPA target. The rest are waste. The brand pays the full invoice regardless. The agency moves on to the next client.

This pricing model made sense when production was expensive and every video required a new shoot, a new creator, a new edit. It does not make sense in 2026, when modular production, AI-assisted editing, and structured testing frameworks let agencies produce 50–100 creative variations from a single shoot day.

The model that replaces it is RaaS UGC — Results as a Service applied to user-generated content production. Instead of paying per deliverable, brands pay for outcomes: conversions, winning creatives, scalable assets. The agency that produces winners earns more. The agency that produces filler earns less.

This is not theoretical. OKAD has been running production systems built for this model across seven campaigns. Here is how it works, what the numbers look like, and how to structure a contract around it.

The Broken Economics of Traditional UGC

The math is brutal. Take a brand spending $300 per UGC video, ordering 20 videos per month. That is $6,000 in production costs. Industry data consistently shows a 15–25% win rate on ad creatives — meaning 3–5 of those 20 videos will outperform the CPA target.

The effective cost per winning creative: $1,200–$2,000.

Now factor in what happens next. The 3–5 winners fatigue within 7–21 days. The brand needs another batch. Another $6,000. Another 15–25% win rate. The cycle repeats monthly, and the brand is effectively paying $1,200–$2,000 for every ad that actually moves the needle.

The problem is not that agencies produce bad work. The problem is that the pricing model has zero feedback loop. There is no mechanism that rewards producing winners or penalizes producing content that never converts. The agency gets paid the same whether a video drives 500 purchases or gets turned off after 48 hours.

What brands actually need

Brands do not need 20 videos. They need 3–5 winners per month that hold up at scale. Everything else in the production pipeline exists to find those winners. The pricing model should reflect that reality.

What RaaS UGC Looks Like: 3 Pricing Models

RaaS is not a single pricing structure. It is a principle — tie compensation to outcomes — that can be implemented in several ways depending on the brand’s spend level, risk tolerance, and measurement maturity.

Model 1: Base + Performance Bonus

The agency charges a reduced base fee (40–60% of traditional per-video pricing) and earns a performance bonus when creatives hit defined KPIs. Example: $120 base per video + $200 bonus for every creative that beats the CPA target for 7+ days.

Best for: Brands spending $15K–$50K/month on media with established CPA benchmarks.

Model 2: Pay Per Conversion

The agency produces creative at cost or near-cost and earns a percentage of revenue or a fixed fee per acquisition driven by their ads. This is the purest form of RaaS — the agency functions almost like a performance marketing partner.

Best for: Brands spending $50K+/month with reliable attribution and willingness to share performance data.

Model 3: Modular Pricing

Break the production pipeline into stages, each priced independently:

  • Pay per test: $X for a structured test batch (e.g., 2 creators x 4 hooks = 8 variations)
  • Pay per winner: Bonus triggered when a creative crosses a performance threshold
  • Pay per scale creative: Additional fee for expanding a winner into new formats, lengths, and platforms

Best for: Brands at any spend level that want granular control over production investment.

Why AI and Modular Production Make RaaS Viable

RaaS UGC was not possible five years ago. The production economics did not support it. Shooting 50 videos to find 5 winners meant 50 separate creator fees, 50 separate edits, 50 separate review cycles. No agency could absorb that risk at sustainable margins.

Three shifts changed the equation:

Modular production. Instead of shooting complete videos, agencies shoot components — hooks, body segments, CTAs, b-roll — and assemble them into dozens of variations. One shoot day produces 30–80 unique ads. The marginal cost of variation number 40 is near zero. Read more in our guide to modular creative production.

AI-assisted editing. Automated editing tools handle cuts, captions, format adaptation (9:16, 1:1, 16:9), and basic motion graphics. What took an editor 2 hours per variation now takes 15 minutes of review time.

Structured testing frameworks. Instead of launching 20 random videos and hoping, agencies run systematic tests that isolate variables — hook vs. hook, angle vs. angle, creator vs. creator. This means faster identification of winners and faster kill decisions on underperformers.

Together, these three factors compress the cost per variation to the point where an agency can profitably produce 50–100 variations, absorb the risk on the 75–85% that underperform, and still earn healthy margins on the winners.

How to Structure a RaaS UGC Contract

A practical RaaS contract needs five components:

1. Base production scope. Define the minimum monthly output — number of test batches, creator slots, and variation targets. This covers the agency’s fixed costs and ensures consistent production cadence.

2. Performance KPIs. Choose 1–2 primary metrics tied to bonuses. CPA and creative win rate are the most common. Avoid vanity metrics like views or impressions unless the campaign is explicitly awareness-focused.

3. Measurement protocol. Agree on attribution windows, reporting tools, and data access. The agency needs access to ad account performance data to optimize. Without it, RaaS is just a pricing gimmick.

4. Bonus thresholds and caps. Define what triggers a bonus (e.g., CPA below $X for 7+ consecutive days) and cap the maximum bonus per period to keep costs predictable for the brand.

5. Review cadence. Monthly or biweekly reviews where both sides analyze what worked, what did not, and what to test next. RaaS without a feedback loop is just discounted production.

Metrics to Tie Compensation To

Not all metrics are equally useful for RaaS contracts. The best metrics are directly influenced by creative quality and resistant to manipulation through media buying tactics.

Tier 1 — Direct performance:

  • CPA (cost per acquisition) — the clearest signal of creative effectiveness
  • ROAS (return on ad spend) — useful when average order values vary
  • Creative win rate — percentage of ads that beat the control or CPA target

Tier 2 — Leading indicators:

  • Hook rate (3-second retention) — predicts whether an ad will get enough data to optimize
  • Thumbstop ratio — the percentage of impressions that result in a pause
  • CTR (click-through rate) — useful when the landing page is not the agency’s responsibility

Tier 3 — Organic metrics (for organic UGC campaigns):

  • Views per post
  • Engagement rate
  • Follower growth attributed to content

Avoid tying compensation to metrics the agency cannot influence: CPM (driven by auction dynamics), reach (driven by budget), or total conversions (driven by budget and audience size, not creative alone).

When RaaS UGC Does Not Work

RaaS is not universal. It fails in specific situations:

Brand awareness campaigns. When there is no conversion event to measure, there is no outcome to tie pricing to. Brand lift studies exist but are expensive and slow — not practical for monthly contract cycles.

New product categories. If no baseline CPA or ROAS exists, you cannot set meaningful performance thresholds. Run 2–3 months of traditional pricing first to establish benchmarks, then transition to RaaS.

Low media spend. Brands spending under $5K/month on paid media generate too few conversions to attribute performance to individual creatives with statistical confidence. At low volumes, a single large order can make a mediocre ad look like a winner.

Brands unwilling to share data. RaaS requires the agency to see what is working and why. If the brand will not grant ad account access or share performance reports, the model collapses.

For these situations, traditional per-video or retainer pricing remains the better option. See our comparison of RaaS vs. retainer models for a detailed breakdown.

Questions people ask

What does RaaS mean in UGC production?

RaaS stands for Results as a Service. Applied to UGC, it means the agency's compensation is tied to measurable outcomes — cost per acquisition, return on ad spend, hook rate, or creative win rate — rather than a flat fee per video. The agency shares the performance risk with the brand.

How is RaaS UGC different from traditional UGC pricing?

Traditional UGC pricing charges $150–$500 per video regardless of performance. The brand absorbs all risk. RaaS UGC uses a lower base production cost plus performance bonuses, pure pay-per-conversion models, or modular pricing where you pay per test, per winner, and per scale creative separately.

What metrics should a RaaS UGC contract use?

The most common metrics are CPA (cost per acquisition), ROAS (return on ad spend), hook rate (3-second retention), thumbstop ratio, and creative win rate (percentage of ads that beat the control). Choose metrics that are directly influenced by creative quality and not distorted by media buying decisions.

When does RaaS UGC not work?

RaaS UGC is a poor fit for brand awareness campaigns with no direct conversion event, new product categories where baselines do not exist yet, and brands spending under $5K/month on media — the sample sizes are too small to attribute performance to individual creatives with confidence.

Can any UGC agency offer RaaS pricing?

Not effectively. RaaS requires modular production systems that generate high volumes at low marginal cost, structured testing frameworks, and enough creative velocity to iterate fast. Agencies that produce 5–10 videos per month cannot absorb the risk. You need the infrastructure to produce 30–100+ variations monthly.
