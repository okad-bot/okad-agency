RaaS vs Retainer: Which Agency Model Actually Works for Ad Creative?

A direct comparison of Results-as-a-Service and retainer agency models for ad creative production. Cost structures, risk profiles, real case data, and a decision framework for CMOs and brand managers.

Guide10 min readby the OKAD team

Every brand paying an agency for ad creative faces the same structural question: should you pay for time or pay for output? The retainer model locks in a monthly fee. The Results-as-a-Service (RaaS) model ties cost to deliverables or performance. Both have real advantages. Both have failure modes that waste budget.

This is not a pitch for one model over the other. It is a decision framework — built on actual campaign data — for choosing the model that fits your stage, budget, and goals.

How Each Model Works

Retainer: You pay a fixed monthly fee — typically $3,000 to $15,000+ for ad creative — in exchange for an agreed scope of work. The agency dedicates a team to your account. Deliverables are estimated but not guaranteed per unit.

RaaS: You pay per result. That result might be per creative delivered, per test completed, per conversion generated, or per ROAS threshold achieved. The agency earns only when it produces something measurable.

The difference is not just pricing — it is incentive structure. A retainer incentivizes the agency to retain you as a client. RaaS incentivizes the agency to maximize your results.

The Retainer: Pros, Cons, and Hidden Risks

Where retainers work well

A retainer makes sense when you need a dedicated team that deeply understands your brand. For complex multi-channel campaigns — where creative spans Meta, TikTok, YouTube, email, and landing pages — a retainer gives the agency room to coordinate across channels without nickel-and-diming every deliverable.

Retainers also work for new brand launches. In the first 2-3 months, nobody knows what will perform. You need creative exploration, and a retainer gives the agency space to experiment without the pressure of per-unit accountability on creative that is inherently experimental.

There is also a relationship benefit that is hard to quantify. A retained team learns your brand voice, understands your internal approval process, and builds context over months. That institutional knowledge reduces revision cycles and speeds up production. Replacing a retained team means restarting that learning curve from zero.

Where retainers break down

The structural problem with retainers is the misaligned incentive during performance dips. When your ads stop working, the agency still gets paid the same fee. Their financial incentive is to keep you on retainer — not to solve the problem as fast as possible.

Scope creep is the other killer. Month one, the retainer covers 15 ad variations. By month four, you are asking for 25 variations plus landing page copy plus email sequences — all at the same price. The agency either pushes back (friction) or quietly reduces quality to absorb the extra work.

The third problem is measurement opacity. Many retainer agencies report activity — hours logged, meetings held, concepts presented — rather than output. Without a clear deliverable count, you cannot calculate your effective cost per creative. And if you cannot calculate it, you cannot compare it to alternatives.

The RaaS Model: Pros, Cons, and Hidden Risks

Where RaaS works well

RaaS excels in performance campaigns with clear metrics. When you can define success as a specific CPA, ROAS threshold, or creative volume, a per-result model ensures every dollar of agency spend maps to a measurable outcome.

High-volume creative production is a natural fit. Consider this: the Swiss Skincare project required roughly 30 ads per day. A retainer could not justify that output at a fixed price — the economics do not work. A per-creative RaaS model makes extreme volume viable because both sides know exactly what each unit costs.

The same logic applies to structured testing frameworks. When you run a systematic test — 2 creators multiplied by 4 hooks — it is far easier to price per test than per hour. The brand pays for the test. The agency is incentivized to make each test count.

Where RaaS breaks down

The biggest risk is cherry-picking. When an agency earns per result, there is a natural pull toward safe, proven creative — the kind that generates predictable output but rarely produces a breakthrough. Experimental formats, new angles, and risky creative get deprioritized because the downside for the agency is real.

Per-unit pricing can also be more expensive in aggregate. If you need 50 ads per month and the per-creative fee is $150, that is $7,500 — potentially more than a retainer that includes the same volume. RaaS trades cost efficiency for accountability.

There is also the tracking burden. RaaS only works when both sides agree on what counts as a “result” and have the infrastructure to measure it. If your attribution is messy — and most brands’ attribution is messier than they think — disputes over what the agency actually delivered become a recurring friction point.

Real Numbers: What the Data Shows

Abstract model comparisons are only useful until you see how the math works in practice. Here is what each model looks like against actual campaign data:

Volume-driven work favors RaaS. The Swiss Skincare campaign produced approximately 30 ads per day across formats and variations. At that volume, a fixed retainer either undercharges the agency (who then cuts corners) or overcharges the brand (who pays for capacity they may not always need). Per-creative pricing keeps both sides honest.

Structured testing maps cleanly to per-unit pricing. The Women’s Health supplement project used a hook testing framework — 2 creators, 4 hooks — producing systematic variations. Each test batch is a discrete unit of work with a clear deliverable. Pricing per test is simpler and fairer than estimating hours.

Clear CPA targets make RaaS measurable. The Longevity supplement campaign achieved EUR 183 CPA. That number is a direct line between creative spend and business outcome. A brand can calculate exactly what each creative dollar produces — which is the entire point of outcome-based pricing.

Re-edits and iterations are natural per-unit work. The Five Brands re-edit project delivered 80 re-edits across multiple brands. Pay per ad delivered, not per hour of editing. The brand knows the cost before work starts. The agency is incentivized to be efficient.

When Retainer Still Wins

Do not switch to RaaS just because it sounds more modern. A retainer is the better choice when:

  • You are launching a new brand or product. The first 60-90 days are exploration. You do not yet know what “results” look like, so you cannot define per-unit success metrics.
  • You need deep strategic partnership. If the agency is involved in brand positioning, market research, and creative strategy — not just production — a retainer gives them room to think beyond the next deliverable.
  • Your creative needs are complex and interconnected. Multi-channel campaigns where email creative informs ad creative which informs landing page copy are hard to decompose into discrete units.
  • Volume is consistent and predictable. If you need exactly 20-30 ads per month, every month, a retainer with clear deliverables is simpler to manage than per-unit invoicing.

When RaaS Wins

RaaS is the stronger choice when:

  • Performance is the primary goal. You are optimizing for CPA, ROAS, or conversion rate — and you want agency compensation tied to those outcomes.
  • You need variable volume. Some months you need 15 ads, other months you need 60. RaaS flexes with demand without renegotiating contracts.
  • You are scaling an existing product. The creative playbook exists. You know what works. Now you need more of it, fast, and you want to pay for output.
  • You have robust tracking in place. RaaS requires clear attribution. Without proper tracking, you cannot verify results, and the model collapses into disputes.

The Hybrid Model: Base Retainer + Performance Layer

Most mature brand-agency relationships land somewhere between pure retainer and pure RaaS. The hybrid model works like this:

Base retainer ($2,000-$5,000/month) covers: strategy sessions, reporting, account management, creative direction, and a minimum deliverable threshold (for example, 10 ad variations per month).

Performance layer adds: per-creative fees above the minimum, bonuses tied to CPA improvement or ROAS thresholds, and surge pricing for high-volume sprints.

This structure gives the agency predictable revenue for planning and hiring while tying the upside to your results. The brand gets a dedicated partner with aligned incentives.

How to structure the performance layer

The performance component can take several forms depending on what matters most to your business:

  • Per-creative fee: $100-$300 per additional ad variation above the retainer minimum. Simple, transparent, easy to forecast.
  • CPA improvement bonus: If the agency beats your target CPA by a defined margin, they earn a percentage of the savings. This works best when you have 3+ months of baseline CPA data.
  • ROAS threshold bonus: Agency earns a bonus when campaign ROAS exceeds an agreed target. Aligns incentives with revenue, not just output.
  • Sprint pricing: A flat per-sprint fee for high-volume production bursts — product launches, seasonal campaigns, or testing sprints that exceed normal cadence.

The key is defining the performance metric before work begins, not after. Retroactive performance negotiations breed resentment on both sides.

How to Transition from Retainer to RaaS

If you are currently on a retainer and considering a shift, do not switch overnight. Follow these steps:

  1. Baseline your current output. Track exactly what your retainer produces for 2-3 months: number of ads, number of tests, turnaround time, performance metrics. You need this data to set fair per-unit pricing.
  2. Define your units. What is a “result” for your business? An ad delivered? A test completed? A CPA target hit? Be specific. Vague definitions create billing disputes.
  3. Propose a hybrid first. Reduce your retainer by 30-50% and add a per-deliverable layer. This gives both sides a transition period.
  4. Set a review cadence. Monthly reviews for the first quarter. Adjust pricing, scope, and metrics based on actual data.
  5. Move to full RaaS only when both sides have enough data. After 3-6 months of hybrid, you will know whether pure RaaS works for your volume and complexity.

One important note on timing: do not start this transition during your busiest season. The first month of any new pricing model involves friction — invoice format changes, scope clarifications, approval workflow adjustments. Run the transition during a period when both sides have bandwidth to work through the kinks.

Red Flags in Both Models

Retainer red flags

  • The agency cannot tell you exactly how many deliverables you received last month
  • Scope has expanded but the fee has not been renegotiated
  • Performance has declined for 4+ weeks with no strategic pivot proposed
  • You are paying for “availability” but the team is clearly juggling too many accounts

RaaS red flags

  • The agency only proposes safe, proven creative formats and resists experimentation
  • Per-unit pricing keeps increasing without corresponding quality improvement
  • Turnaround times stretch because the agency prioritizes higher-paying clients
  • No transparency into the production process — you only see the final deliverable

Questions to Ask Before Signing Either Model

Regardless of which direction you lean, these questions cut through the sales pitch:

For retainer agencies: How many deliverables did your average client receive last month? What happens when we need to scale volume by 2x for a product launch? How do you handle scope changes mid-month? What is your average client tenure, and why did your last three clients leave?

For RaaS agencies: What counts as a “deliverable” and who decides if it meets the quality bar? How do you handle experimental creative that may not perform? What is your minimum commitment, and what happens if we need fewer deliverables than expected? How do you prevent cherry-picking easy wins over strategic bets?

The answers tell you more about the agency than the model itself.

Making the Decision

The right model depends on your stage, not your preference. Early-stage brands with undefined creative playbooks benefit from retainer flexibility. Growth-stage brands with proven creative and clear metrics benefit from RaaS accountability. Most brands in the middle benefit from a hybrid that evolves over time.

Whatever model you choose, the non-negotiable is measurement. Track output, track cost-per-unit, track performance-per-creative. Without that data, both models are just different ways to pay for uncertainty.

The best brand-agency relationships evolve their pricing model as the relationship matures. Start where you are. Measure everything. Adjust the model when the data tells you to.

Questions people ask

What is RaaS (Results as a Service) in agency pricing?

RaaS is a pricing model where you pay an agency per deliverable or per outcome — per ad creative delivered, per conversion generated, or per ROAS threshold met. Unlike retainer models where you pay a fixed monthly fee regardless of output, RaaS ties your cost directly to measurable results. This aligns incentives: the agency only earns when it produces.

Is a retainer or RaaS model cheaper for ad creative?

Neither is universally cheaper. Retainers offer a lower per-unit cost when volume is high and consistent. RaaS has a higher per-unit cost but eliminates waste — you never pay for idle capacity. For brands spending $5K-$15K/month on creative, a retainer is typically more cost-efficient. For brands that need flexible volume or want strict cost-per-result accountability, RaaS often delivers better ROI despite higher unit prices.

Can I combine a retainer and RaaS model with the same agency?

Yes. A hybrid model uses a smaller base retainer for core services (strategy, reporting, account management) and adds a per-deliverable or performance-bonus layer on top. This gives the agency predictable revenue for planning while keeping incentives aligned with your results. Many brands start with a pure retainer and transition to hybrid as they build enough data to define clear output metrics.

What are the biggest risks of each model?

With retainers, the main risk is paying full price during slow periods or when performance drops — the agency has no financial incentive to course-correct quickly. With RaaS, the risk is that the agency cherry-picks easy wins and avoids experimental creative that could be a breakthrough. Both risks are manageable with the right contract structure and reporting cadence.

How do I transition from a retainer to a performance-based agency model?

Start by tracking your current retainer's output in concrete units: ads delivered, tests run, conversion improvements. Once you have 2-3 months of baseline data, you can define fair per-unit pricing. Then propose a hybrid — keep a reduced retainer for strategy and add a per-deliverable fee. This gives both sides a transition period before moving to full RaaS.
