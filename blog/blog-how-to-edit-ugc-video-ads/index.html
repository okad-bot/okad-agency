How to Edit UGC Video Ads: From Raw Footage to Scroll-Stopper

Step-by-step UGC video editing workflow — from importing raw creator footage to exporting platform-ready ads. Covers pacing, captions, hook editing, B-roll, and export settings for Meta, TikTok, and YouTube Shorts.

Guide10 min readby the OKAD team

The footage is never the ad. A creator sends you three minutes of raw takes — some good, some shaky, some gold buried under filler. What turns that into a scroll-stopping ad is the edit.

Editing is where hooks get sharpened, pacing gets tightened, and a rambling testimonial becomes a 22-second conversion machine. Most brands either over-edit UGC into something that looks like a commercial, or under-edit it into something no one watches past second two.

This guide covers the full editing workflow from raw footage to platform-ready ad — with the specific pacing rules, tool settings, and export specs that separate ads people watch from ads people skip.

The Tools

Pick one and learn it well. Switching tools costs more time than any feature difference saves.

CapCut (Free) — Best for speed. Auto-captions, built-in aspect ratio presets, native TikTok export. Desktop version is significantly better than mobile. Use this if you edit fewer than 50 ads per month or work primarily on TikTok and Reels.

Premiere Pro ($23/month) — Best for scale. Multi-sequence workflows, batch export via Media Encoder, robust plugin ecosystem. Use this if you edit at volume, need team collaboration, or work across formats (video ads, YouTube content, long-form).

DaVinci Resolve (Free / $295 one-time) — Best for color. Industry-leading color grading tools in the free version. Use this when visual consistency across a batch matters — product shots that need to match, skin tones that need to feel natural.

After Effects ($23/month) — Not an editing tool. Use it only for motion graphics overlays — animated logos, kinetic text, product callouts — then export those as assets to drop into your timeline in Premiere or CapCut.

Step 1: Import and Organize

Before you touch the timeline, sort the raw footage:

Create a folder structure per project: /Raw, /Selects, /Audio, /Graphics, /Exports. Drop all creator footage into Raw. Watch everything once at 1.5x speed. Mark usable takes — flag them, star them, move them to Selects. Most raw footage is 60-70% unusable. That is normal.

Label your selects by type: hook-01, hook-02, demo-unbox, demo-apply, testimonial-results, broll-product, broll-lifestyle. This labeling system is what makes modular production possible — you are building a component library, not editing a single video.

Step 2: Build the Rough Cut

Every UGC ad follows one of five structures. Pick the one that matches your footage:

Talking Head: Hook → Problem → Product intro → Demo → Result → CTA. The creator carries the entire ad with their face and voice. Fastest to edit — mostly straight cuts with B-roll inserts over product mentions.

Product Demo: Hook (visual) → Product reveal → Feature walkthrough → Before/after or result → CTA. Less talking, more showing. Requires tight B-roll and clear product shots.

Before/After: Hook (pain point) → Before state → Transition → After state → Product credit → CTA. The transition is the money shot. Split screen, whip pan, or hard cut all work — match the energy of the transformation.

Story Ad: Hook (curiosity) → Setup → Conflict/problem → Discovery → Resolution → CTA. Longer format (30-45 seconds). Needs strong pacing to hold attention through the narrative arc.

Compilation/Mashup: Hook (bold claim) → Creator 1 clip → Creator 2 clip → Creator 3 clip → Social proof overlay → CTA. Rapid cuts between multiple creators. Works best when you have 3-5 creators saying variations of the same message.

Lay down your selects on the timeline in the rough structure. Do not worry about timing yet. Get the story right first.

Step 3: Edit the Hook (First 1-3 Seconds)

The hook is 80% of whether your ad works. Edit it separately from the rest.

Rule: The first visual change must happen within 1 second. Not 2 seconds. Not 1.5. One second or less. This can be a cut, a text pop, a zoom, a product appearing in frame — any visual event that tells the viewer something is happening.

Text hooks — Drop a bold text overlay on frame 1. “I stopped buying [product category]” or “This replaced my entire routine.” The text appears before the creator even speaks. Viewers read faster than they listen.

Visual hooks — Start with the most visually arresting frame. Not the intro, not the setup — the moment that makes someone stop scrolling. A product transformation, an unexpected result, a striking before/after.

Audio hooks — The first word matters. “Stop,” “Wait,” “Okay so,” “I was wrong about” — conversational openers that create a micro-commitment. Cut any dead air or “um” before the first word. The audio should start on frame 1.

This is the principle behind the Hook Swap System — the same ad body with different hooks creates structurally different ads. When OKAD produces roughly 30 ads per day for a skincare brand, most of that volume comes from hook editing, not reshooting.

Step 4: Tighten the Pacing

UGC ads die when they breathe too long. Every platform has a different tolerance:

TikTok / Reels: 2-second average shot length. Cut aggressively. Jump cuts are native to the format — do not smooth them with transitions. If a shot can be shorter, it should be.

Meta Feed (4:5, 1:1): 3-4 second average shot length. Slightly more room to let moments land. Still cut any shot that holds longer than 4 seconds without new visual information.

YouTube Shorts: 2-3 second average. Closer to TikTok pacing but allows slightly longer product demos because the audience expects more substance.

Universal rules:

  • Cut on action — when the creator’s hand moves, when the product enters frame, when there is a gesture. Never cut in dead space.
  • Remove every pause longer than 0.5 seconds. Tighten gaps between sentences to 0.2-0.3 seconds.
  • If a sentence can be split across two shots (A-roll to B-roll and back), split it. The visual change keeps attention.
  • Watch your edit at 1x speed with sound. If your attention drifts at any point, cut that section shorter.

Step 5: Insert B-Roll

B-roll turns a talking head into a dynamic ad. Insert it over three types of moments:

Product mentions — Every time the creator names the product, cut to a product shot. Close-up, lifestyle angle, or in-use footage. This reinforces the product visually at the exact moment the viewer hears about it.

Claims and results — “My skin cleared up in two weeks” should cut to the clear skin. “I saved 3 hours” should cut to a screen recording or graphic. Never let a claim exist only as words.

Pacing breaks — If a talking-head section runs longer than 5 seconds, insert 1-2 seconds of B-roll to reset visual attention. The audio continues; only the visual changes.

The re-edit approach that produced 80 ads from existing footage across five brands is essentially B-roll architecture — reorganizing the same visual components into new sequences that the algorithm treats as fresh creative.

Step 6: Add Captions and Text

Captions are not optional. 85% of social video is watched without sound.

Auto-generate first. CapCut and Premiere both transcribe speech automatically. Always review the output — auto-captions mangle brand names, product terms, and accented speech.

Style rules:

  • 2-4 words per line maximum
  • Semi-transparent background bar (not full black — it looks like a funeral)
  • Bold or highlight the key word in each phrase: “I stopped buying foundation”
  • Position: bottom third for talking head, top third if there is a product in the lower frame
  • Font: clean sans-serif, 40-60pt equivalent. No script fonts, no all-caps unless it is a single emphasis word

Text overlays (not captions): Use sparingly for hook text, key stats, price points, or CTA reinforcement. Animate with a simple pop or fade — no spinning, bouncing, or flying text. Motion graphics belong in brand ads, not UGC.

Step 7: Audio and Sound Design

Level your audio. Raw UGC comes in at wildly inconsistent volumes. Normalize all speech to -6dB to -3dB. Background music should sit at -18dB to -14dB — audible but never competing with the voice.

Music selection: Use trending sounds on TikTok (check the Creative Center). For Meta ads, use royalty-free tracks — Epidemic Sound, Artlist, or the Meta Sound Collection. Match energy to the ad: upbeat for product reveals, ambient for testimonials, percussive for before/after transformations.

Sound effects: A subtle whoosh on cuts, a soft pop on text appearances, a click on CTA buttons. These are felt more than heard. One or two per ad maximum. More than that turns UGC into a motion graphics reel.

Step 8: Color Grade for Authenticity

The goal is not cinematic color. The goal is consistent, natural-looking footage that does not scream “edited.”

  • White balance — Fix any footage that is too warm (orange) or too cold (blue). Match all clips to the same approximate color temperature. In DaVinci Resolve, use the eyedropper on a white or neutral gray object in frame.
  • Exposure — Lift shadows slightly so dark areas are not crushed. Pull highlights down if skin is blown out. UGC is shot in uncontrolled lighting, so exposure correction is almost always needed. Target skin tones between 60-70 IRE on the waveform.
  • Contrast — Add a small S-curve: lift the blacks slightly (so they are dark gray, not pure black) and pull the whites down a touch. This creates the “phone camera” feel that audiences associate with authentic content.
  • Saturation — Add 5-10% at most. Over-saturated UGC looks fake immediately. If anything, slightly desaturated footage reads as more authentic.
  • Skin tones — Check the vectorscope. Skin tones should fall along the skin tone line regardless of ethnicity. If they drift toward green or magenta, use the hue-vs-hue curve to correct.
  • Match between clips — When you cut between different creators or different days of footage, the color difference is jarring. Use shot matching in Resolve or Lumetri color match in Premiere to bring all clips into the same visual family.
  • Do not add film grain, LUTs, or heavy color stylization. These destroy the UGC aesthetic that makes the format work. The only exception: a very subtle grain (1-2%) can mask compression artifacts on heavily re-encoded footage.

At scale, the fastest path to volume is always hook swaps and format crops on proven base edits — not building new edits from scratch. This is how production at the re-edit level reaches 80 ads without a single new shoot.

The Editing Mindset

Editing UGC is not post-production. It is production. The raw footage is your material; the ad is what you build from it.

The brands scaling fastest on paid social treat editing as their primary creative lever. One shoot day, organized into modular components, edited into 50+ variations with different hooks, pacing, and structures. Each variation is a new test. Each test generates data. The data informs the next round of edits.

This is not about making content look good. It is about making content that stops thumbs, holds attention, and drives action — in that order, in the first 3 seconds, in the edit room.

Questions people ask

What is the best free editing tool for UGC ads?

CapCut is the best free option for UGC ad editing. It runs on desktop and mobile, includes auto-captions, has built-in templates sized for TikTok and Reels, and exports without a watermark. For more advanced work — color grading, multi-cam sync, or complex compositing — DaVinci Resolve offers a free tier that rivals paid tools.

How long does it take to edit one UGC video ad?

A simple talking-head UGC ad with captions takes 20-40 minutes once you have a workflow. A more complex edit — hook swaps, B-roll inserts, motion text, sound design — takes 1-2 hours. Editors working at scale with templates and presets can cut that to 15-30 minutes per variation. The Hook Swap System produces new ads in under 10 minutes each because only the first 2-3 seconds change.

Should UGC ads look polished or raw?

Somewhere in between. Viewers scroll past content that looks like a commercial, but they also skip content that looks unwatchable. The goal is intentional imperfection — clean audio, readable captions, and a clear message, but with the handheld feel, natural lighting, and conversational tone that signals authenticity. Color grade lightly. Do not smooth skin. Keep jump cuts instead of hiding them with transitions.

How do I add captions to UGC video ads?

CapCut and Premiere Pro both have auto-caption tools that transcribe speech and sync text to the timeline. After auto-generating, always review for accuracy — especially brand names and product terms. Style captions with a semi-transparent background bar for readability. Use 2-4 words per line maximum. Bold or color the key word in each phrase to guide the eye.

What are the most common UGC editing mistakes?

Five mistakes kill most UGC ads: over-editing with transitions and effects that destroy the authentic feel; leaving the hook too slow (the first visual change must happen within 1 second); inconsistent audio levels between clips; exporting in the wrong aspect ratio for the platform; and cutting too little — most raw UGC is 2-3x longer than the final ad should be. When in doubt, cut more.
