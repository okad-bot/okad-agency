AI Video Prompts: How to Get Results Faster

Practical AI video prompt strategies that eliminate trial and error. Key frame method, prompt libraries, and artifact avoidance from real production campaigns.

Guide10 min readby the OKAD team

The standard approach to AI video goes like this: write a detailed description of what you want to see, hit generate, review the output, rewrite the prompt, generate again, adjust phrasing, generate again, try different wording, generate again. After 10-15 attempts, you might have something usable. More likely, you have something that is close enough and you settle.

This approach is slow, unpredictable, and produces content that looks like AI generated it — because you let the AI make most of the visual decisions.

There is a faster method. It inverts the process: instead of describing what you want in words and hoping the AI interprets correctly, you build the visual endpoints and let the AI fill the motion between them. This article covers the practical techniques that cut iteration time by 70-80%, drawn from production campaigns spanning jewelry, publishing, and app marketing.

The Fundamental Problem With Text Prompts

Text prompts fail at AI video for a specific reason: video is spatial and temporal, while text is sequential. When you write “a ruby ring rotating slowly on a dark marble surface with soft side lighting,” you are describing a scene with dozens of visual parameters — camera angle, rotation speed, rotation axis, marble texture, marble color, light direction, light intensity, ring proportions, stone faceting, depth of field.

The AI has to make decisions about every parameter you did not specify. And it makes different decisions each time. That is why the same prompt produces wildly different results across generations.

The more visual decisions you leave to the AI, the more iterations you need.

The Key Frame Solution

The key frame method eliminates ambiguity by giving the AI visual instructions instead of verbal ones.

A key frame is a hand-composed still image — built from real assets, photography, or illustration — that defines what a specific moment should look like. For AI video, you provide two key frames: where the clip starts and where it ends. The AI generates the motion between them.

What this changes in practice:

The prompt becomes simple: “Animate the transition from Frame A to Frame B with slow, steady motion.” The AI’s job shrinks from “imagine an entire scene” to “create movement between two defined points.” This is why key frames reduce iteration from 8-15 attempts to 2-4.

Building Effective Key Frames

Key frames are not rough mockups. They are finished still images with the same level of care as a final photograph or illustration. The quality of your key frame directly determines the quality of the AI output.

Source Material First

Start with real assets. For a jewelry client, we photographed rings, stones, and settings, then composed key frames from these photographs. For a book publisher, we used the artist’s original illustrations. For a product brand, we used their existing product photography.

AI-generated source material introduces compounding errors — you are asking the AI to animate from an AI-generated starting point, doubling the opportunity for artifacts and drift.

Composition Rules

Three rules that consistently produce better output:

1. Never center the subject. AI video generators default to centered, symmetrical compositions. This reads as generated. Place the primary subject off-center using rough thirds. When the AI interpolates motion from an off-center start to an off-center end, the resulting movement has the compositional variety of a tracked camera shot rather than a static locked-off frame.

2. Matte and imperfect textures. AI renders surfaces as plastic-smooth and reflective by default. Build key frames with matte surfaces, visible texture, and deliberate imperfection. A marble surface should have veining and slight discoloration. A fabric should have weave texture. Skin should have pores. These details survive the animation process and prevent the output from looking synthetic.

3. Side lighting, not front lighting. Front lighting (light source behind the camera) flattens depth and reads as studio-generic. Side lighting creates shadows, depth, and visual interest. It also gives the AI more information about the three-dimensional structure of the scene, which produces more convincing motion.

For our jewelry client, these three rules — never centered, matte/imperfect, side light — governed 12-18 posts and videos monthly across 6-7 months of production. The consistency came from the rules, not from the AI.

The Prompt Library Approach

When you produce content regularly for the same brand or product category, the most impactful optimization is a prompt library — a documented set of tested configurations mapped to content types.

How We Built One for Jewelry

The jewelry client required content featuring 10+ different stone types: rubies, emeralds, sapphires, opals, diamonds, pearls, and several semi-precious varieties. Each stone interacts with light differently. A ruby absorbs and re-emits red light. An opal refracts multiple colors. A diamond creates sharp white reflections.

A single generic “rotating gemstone” prompt cannot handle this variance. We built a prompt per stone type, tested over multiple generations, documenting:

  • Optimal lighting angle for that stone’s refraction
  • Motion speed (faster for flashy stones like diamonds, slower for deep-color stones like garnets)
  • Background surface that best contrasts the stone’s color
  • Camera distance and depth of field
  • Any stone-specific artifacts to watch for (e.g., opals tended to lose their play-of-color mid-animation)

The result was a prompt library that turned a 30-minute iteration process per clip into a 5-minute selection and generation process. Over 6-7 months of producing 12-18 posts and videos monthly, this library saved hundreds of hours.

Library Structure

A practical prompt library maps three dimensions:

Content type — product showcase, lifestyle context, detail close-up, comparison, unboxing Subject — each product, character, or visual element with its own tuned settings Platform — Instagram (1:1 and 9:16), TikTok (9:16), X (16:9), YouTube (16:9)

Each intersection gets a tested prompt configuration. Not every combination needs unique settings — many share defaults — but the exceptions are documented so no one has to rediscover them.

Avoiding AI Artifacts

AI video has a recognizable set of defects. Learning to identify and prevent them saves more time than any prompt technique.

Plastic Skin

AI renders human skin as smooth, pore-free, and uniformly lit. In video, this manifests as a waxy, mannequin-like quality that viewers register as uncanny even if they cannot name the problem.

Prevention: Build key frames with visible skin texture. Use photographic reference images where pores, subtle blemishes, and uneven skin tone are present. The AI interpolates from what you give it — if the source has texture, the output retains more of it.

Perfect Symmetry

AI gravitates toward bilateral symmetry in faces, objects, and compositions. Real-world scenes are never perfectly symmetrical. The effect is subtle but cumulative — a perfectly symmetrical face in motion reads as animated rather than alive.

Prevention: Introduce asymmetry in key frames. Slightly different lighting on each side of the face. One earring visible, the other partially hidden. A composition that is weighted to one side. These small interventions break the AI’s symmetry bias.

Color Discontinuity

Across multiple clips in a sequence, AI drifts in color temperature, saturation, and contrast. A warm-toned clip might be followed by a cool-toned clip, creating a jarring visual shift.

Prevention: The key frame method addresses this directly. When both start and end frames share a matched color palette, the AI’s interpolation stays within the correct color space. For sequences of multiple clips, build key frames with overlapping endpoints — the end frame of clip 1 is the start frame of clip 2. This chains color consistency through the entire sequence.

Drifting or Warping Text

AI cannot reliably maintain text stability during animation. Letters warp, drift, and occasionally transform into different characters.

Prevention: Never include text in animated segments. Add all text during holds — static frames in post-production where no motion occurs. This is a hard rule with no exceptions. If a frame has text, that frame does not move.

Background Instability

During character or object animation, AI sometimes warps the background — walls bend, surfaces ripple, objects in the background shift position.

Prevention: Use simple, low-contrast backgrounds in key frames. A solid dark surface is more stable than a detailed environment. When complex backgrounds are necessary, use holds for the detailed environment and reserve animation for simpler compositions.

Practical Workflow: From Brief to Final Clip

Here is the complete workflow for producing a single AI video clip with minimal iteration:

Step 1: Define the clip’s purpose. What action or transformation should the viewer see? Not “a beautiful video of a ring” but “the ring rotates 90 degrees, catching light on the primary stone.”

Step 2: Source visual assets. Gather product photography, illustrations, or real-world reference images. No AI-generated source material.

Step 3: Build start and end key frames. Hand-compose two still images that define the clip’s beginning and end. Apply the three rules: off-center subject, matte textures, side lighting.

Step 4: Write a minimal prompt. The prompt should describe only the motion — “slow clockwise rotation,” “gentle camera pull-back,” “character shifts weight from left to right.” The visual content is already defined by the key frames.

Step 5: Generate and select. Generate 2-4 clips. Select the one with the smoothest motion, best color consistency, and fewest artifacts. If none are usable, review the key frames — the issue is usually there, not in the prompt.

Step 6: Post-production. Add holds for text, apply color correction for consistency with other clips, add sound design.

This process produces a usable clip in 20-40 minutes. The text-prompt-only approach averages 2-3 hours for comparable quality.

When to Start With Prompts vs Key Frames

Key frames are not always necessary. For simple motion — a slow camera pan across a static image, a gentle zoom, a subtle particle effect — a text prompt with a single reference image can work on the first or second try.

The decision framework:

Text prompt sufficient: Simple camera motion, single static subject, no character or face, abstract or atmospheric content, short clips (under 3 seconds).

Key frames recommended: Character animation, product rotation or transformation, multi-element scenes, content requiring brand consistency, any sequence longer than 6 seconds (composed of multiple clips), content featuring faces or hands.

Prompt library recommended: Any project producing more than 10 pieces of content per month, recurring content with the same subjects, campaigns spanning multiple months, brands with strict visual guidelines.

The investment in key frames and prompt libraries pays for itself within the first month of regular production. The time saved per clip multiplies across every piece of content produced, and the quality improvement compounds as the library grows more refined through use.

Questions people ask

What is the best way to prompt AI video generators?

The most effective approach is not better text prompts — it is better visual inputs. The key frame method gives the AI a hand-built start and end frame, reducing the prompt to motion direction rather than scene description. This eliminates 70-80% of the iteration that text-only prompting requires.

How many iterations does it take to get a good AI video clip?

With text-only prompting, expect 8-15 attempts per usable clip. With the key frame method (hand-built start and end frames), expect 2-4 attempts. The difference is that key frames remove ambiguity — the AI is not interpreting a description, it is filling motion between two specific images.

How do you avoid the AI look in generated videos?

Three rules: never center the subject, use matte and imperfect textures instead of glossy renders, and light from the side rather than the front. AI defaults to centered, symmetrical, studio-lit output. Deliberately breaking these defaults produces content that looks composed by a human rather than generated by a machine.

Should I build a prompt library for AI video?

Yes, if you produce content regularly. A prompt library maps each content type to tested settings — camera angle, lighting direction, motion style, texture treatment. For a jewelry client with 10+ stone types, we built prompts tuned per stone. This turned a 30-minute iteration process into a 5-minute selection process for each new piece.
