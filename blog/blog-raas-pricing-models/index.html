RaaS Pricing Models: How to Charge for Results Instead of Hours

Five practical RaaS pricing models with real examples — per-outcome, base-plus-performance, gain share, tiered results, and credit-based. Includes formulas, margins, and when to use each model.

Guide8 min readby the OKAD team

The hardest part of launching Results as a Service is not the concept — it is the pricing. How do you set a price per outcome that is fair to the client, profitable for you, and sustainable at scale?

This guide covers five RaaS pricing models used by real companies in 2026. Each model has different risk profiles, margin structures, and ideal use cases. Pick the one that matches your operational efficiency, client base, and risk tolerance.

Model 1: Per-Outcome Pricing

The purest form of RaaS. You deliver a service unit. If it hits the defined outcome, you get paid. If it does not, you do not.

How it works:

  • Define the outcome: ad beats CPA target, support ticket resolved, lead qualified, article ranks on page 1
  • Set a price per successful outcome
  • Track and invoice based on achieved outcomes

Real examples:

  • Salesforce Agentforce: $2 per AI agent conversation
  • Zendesk AI: $1.50 per resolved interaction
  • Recruitment agencies: 15–25% of first-year salary per successful hire

For creative agencies: $200–$600 per ad creative that achieves below-target CPA within a defined measurement window. OKAD’s production model supports this structure — when you produce 80+ re-edits from existing footage at low marginal cost, the economics of per-outcome pricing work because winners more than cover the cost of non-performers.

The math:

Revenue = Outcome price × Number of successful outcomes
Cost = Production cost × Total units produced (winners + non-winners)
Margin = Revenue − Cost

If you charge $400 per winning ad, produce 100 ads at $90 each, and 38 win:

  • Revenue: $400 × 38 = $15,200
  • Cost: $90 × 100 = $9,000
  • Margin: $6,200 (41%)

When to use: High-volume services with clear, binary outcome definitions. You need enough volume (30+ units/month) for the success rate to stabilize.

When to avoid: Low volume, long feedback loops (results take months to materialize), or outcomes influenced by factors outside your control.

Model 2: Base Plus Performance

The most popular RaaS structure because it balances risk between provider and client.

How it works:

  • Base fee: reduced fixed monthly fee covering operational costs (team, tools, infrastructure)
  • Performance fee: variable component tied to outcome metrics
  • Total compensation = Base + (Performance rate × Outcomes achieved)

Structure variations:

SplitBase (% of total)Performance (% of total)Risk Profile
Conservative60%40%Low risk, moderate upside
Balanced50%50%Equal risk sharing
Aggressive30%70%High risk, high upside
Pure performance0%100%Maximum risk (rare)

Example for a creative agency:

  • Base: $4,000/month (covers 2 team members, tools, strategy)
  • Performance: $200 per ad that beats the CPA benchmark
  • Month with 20 winners: $4,000 + $4,000 = $8,000
  • Month with 35 winners: $4,000 + $7,000 = $11,000
  • Month with 10 winners: $4,000 + $2,000 = $6,000

The client pays less in bad months and more in good months. The agency has a floor that covers costs. Both sides are incentivized to maximize performance.

When to use: Transitioning from retainers, first-time RaaS clients, services where outcomes take 2–4 weeks to measure.

When to avoid: Clients who will try to renegotiate the base fee down to zero over time. Protect it contractually.

Model 3: Gain Share / Revenue Share

You take a percentage of the value your work creates.

How it works:

  • Measure the revenue or savings attributable to your work
  • Take a percentage as compensation
  • Typically measured against a baseline (pre-engagement performance)

Example:

  • Client’s ad spend: $50,000/month
  • Baseline ROAS before your involvement: 2.5x
  • After your creative optimization: 3.8x
  • Revenue increase: $50,000 × (3.8 − 2.5) = $65,000 additional revenue
  • Your share at 10%: $6,500/month

When to use: Deep, exclusive partnerships where you have significant influence over outcomes. Works best when both parties share full revenue data transparently.

When to avoid: Clients with multiple agencies (attribution becomes disputed), short engagements (no time to prove impact), or services where your contribution is one of many factors in the result.

The attribution problem: Gain share requires both parties to agree on what caused the revenue change. Was it your creative? The media buyer’s optimization? A seasonal trend? A competitor leaving the market? Attribution disputes kill gain share agreements. Use only when your impact is clearly isolatable.

Model 4: Tiered Results Pricing

Different prices for different levels of performance.

How it works:

  • Tier 1 (Delivered): Deliverable meets quality standards and launches → base production fee
  • Tier 2 (Performing): Deliverable hits the performance benchmark → standard outcome fee
  • Tier 3 (Exceptional): Deliverable is a top performer (top 10–15%) → premium fee

Example for creative production:

TierDefinitionPrice per UnitTypical Volume (of 100 ads)
DeliveredApproved, launched$75100 (all)
PerformingCPA below target$25030–40
ExceptionalTop 10% by ROAS$5008–12

Monthly revenue from 100 ads:

  • Tier 1: $75 × 100 = $7,500
  • Tier 2: $250 × 35 = $8,750
  • Tier 3: $500 × 10 = $5,000
  • Total: $21,250

Compare this to a flat retainer of $15,000/month for the same volume. When your work performs well, tiered pricing earns more. When it underperforms, it earns less — which is the point. The model rewards quality.

When to use: Creative services, content production, lead generation — anything with a natural performance distribution.

When to avoid: Services where quality is binary (done/not done) with no meaningful performance gradient.

Model 5: Credit-Based / Consumption Pricing

Clients purchase credits upfront and spend them as they consume your service.

How it works:

  • Client buys a credit package ($5,000 for 50 credits)
  • Each outcome consumes credits based on complexity or value
  • Simple deliverable: 1 credit. Complex deliverable: 3 credits. High-value outcome: 5 credits.
  • Credits expire monthly or quarterly, incentivizing usage

Example:

  • 50 credits at $100 each = $5,000 prepaid
  • Standard ad creative (approved and launched): 1 credit
  • Performance creative (beats CPA target): 2 credits (1 consumed at launch, 1 on performance confirmation)
  • Winning creative for scaling (top performer): 3 credits

When to use: Clients who want budget predictability but outcome accountability. Works well for agencies serving multiple service types within one contract.

When to avoid: Small engagements where credit tracking overhead exceeds the benefit.

Most agencies start with base-plus-performance for their first 3–6 months, then evolve toward tiered results or per-outcome as they build confidence in their metrics and efficiency.

Setting Your Specific Prices

Regardless of model, the pricing math follows the same logic:

Step 1: Calculate Your Cost Per Successful Outcome

Cost per success = Total production cost ÷ Number of successful outcomes

If you produce 100 units at $90 each ($9,000 total) and 35 succeed: Cost per success = $9,000 ÷ 35 = $257

Step 2: Add Your Target Margin

Outcome price = Cost per success ÷ (1 − Target margin)

At 40% target margin: $257 ÷ 0.60 = $428

Step 3: Validate Against Client Value

What is one successful outcome worth to the client? If a winning ad generates $3,000+ in revenue, $428 is easily justified. If it generates $500, you need to lower costs, improve success rate, or accept lower margins.

Step 4: Build in Variance Protection

Your success rate will fluctuate. Price for your worst reasonable month, not your average:

Conservative price = Cost per success at 80% of normal success rate ÷ (1 − Minimum margin)

If your normal success rate is 35% but might dip to 28% in a bad month: Cost per success = $9,000 ÷ 28 = $321 Conservative price = $321 ÷ 0.75 (25% minimum margin) = $428

In this case, both the average and conservative calculations land at similar prices — which means $428 is robust.

Pricing Mistakes That Kill RaaS Businesses

1. Racing to the bottom. Competing on per-outcome price against agencies with lower costs. Compete on success rate instead — a higher price with a better win rate delivers more value.

2. Ignoring non-billable production. You produce 100 ads but only 35 generate outcome revenue. The other 65 still cost money. Your pricing must cover the full production run, not just the winners.

3. Static pricing. Your costs change. Your success rate changes. Market conditions change. Review and adjust pricing quarterly based on actual performance data.

4. No floor in base-plus-performance. The base fee must cover your real operational costs. If the performance component goes to zero for one month (it happens), the base keeps your team employed and your tools running.

5. Measuring too late. If the outcome takes 90 days to measure, your cash flow is 90 days behind your work. Build interim measurement points or shorten the attribution window.

Questions people ask

What is the most common RaaS pricing model?

The base-plus-performance model is the most widely used. It combines a reduced fixed fee (covering operational costs) with a variable component tied to outcomes. Most businesses start here because it provides stability for the provider while giving the client outcome-based accountability. Typical splits range from 50/50 to 40% base / 60% performance.

How do I calculate my per-outcome price?

Start with your cost per successful outcome: divide total production cost by the number of outcomes that hit the target. For example, if producing 100 units costs $10,000 and 35 meet the success criteria, your cost per successful outcome is $286. Add your target margin (typically 30–50%) to get your per-outcome price. In this example: $286 ÷ 0.65 = $440 per successful outcome at 35% margin.

Should I charge the same price for all outcomes?

No. Tiered pricing rewards exceptional results and covers the cost of non-performers. A three-tier structure is common: base tier for deliverables that meet minimum standards, performance tier for those hitting benchmarks, and premium tier for top 10% outcomes. This aligns incentives — you earn more when you deliver more value.

What happens when the client's tracking is wrong?

Measurement disputes are the biggest risk in RaaS. Prevent them by agreeing on the measurement methodology before work begins, using a shared dashboard both parties access, building a 48–72 hour review window before invoices finalize, and including a dispute resolution clause in the contract. If tracking cannot be trusted, do not use RaaS — use a retainer until measurement is reliable.

Can I use RaaS pricing for one-time projects?

RaaS works best for ongoing relationships with recurring deliverables. One-time projects lack the volume needed for statistical smoothing. For a one-time project, consider a milestone-based fee where partial payment triggers at each deliverable stage, with a performance bonus at the end if the project hits its target metric.
