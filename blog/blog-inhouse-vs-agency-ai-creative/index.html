AI Ad Creative: In-House vs Agency vs Freelancer

Decision framework for choosing between in-house AI creative, an agency, or freelancers. Honest pros, cons, costs, and when each option makes sense.

Guide10 min readby the OKAD team

Every brand running paid ads at scale eventually faces the same question: should we build AI creative capability internally, hire an agency, or piece it together with freelancers?

The answer is not obvious, and the wrong choice costs more than money. It costs time — months of suboptimal creative output while your competitors test 10x more variations than you do. This guide lays out a decision framework based on what we have seen work (and fail) across dozens of brands producing AI-assisted ad creative.

The Three Models, Honestly

Before diving into comparisons, here is what each model actually looks like in practice — not the pitch deck version, but the daily reality.

In-House: Full Control, Full Cost

Building an in-house AI creative team means hiring specialists, buying tool subscriptions, developing workflows from scratch, and absorbing every mistake during the learning curve.

What you need at minimum:

  • 1 creative strategist who understands performance marketing
  • 1-2 editors proficient in AI-assisted production tools
  • 1 person managing creator relationships and footage libraries
  • Software subscriptions: editing suite, AI generation tools, asset management, analytics
  • 3-6 months of ramp-up before consistent output quality

Realistic monthly cost: $15-25K in salaries and tools before a single ad goes live.

The real advantage is institutional knowledge. Your team learns your brand voice, your audience, your product nuances — and that knowledge compounds. After 12 months, an in-house team that has survived the learning curve understands your brand better than any external partner.

The real disadvantage is that you are building a production system from zero. Agencies have refined their workflows across dozens of clients. You are figuring it out with one brand — yours — and paying full price for every lesson.

Agency: Production System, Shared Learning

An agency brings a pre-built production machine. The workflows, tools, quality controls, and testing methodology already exist. You are renting a system that was built and refined across multiple clients.

For example, when a Swiss skincare brand needed to scale ad production, the agency system produced roughly 30 ads per day from a single UGC library — including a Hook Swap System that replaced underperforming hooks without re-editing the entire ad body. That kind of systematic approach kept orders stable for 2-3 months straight. Building that system internally would have taken the brand months of trial and error.

Realistic monthly cost: $5-15K depending on volume and service scope.

The real advantage is speed to output and cross-pollination. Agencies learn what works across categories. A hook testing methodology refined on a dating app campaign might inform a supplement brand’s creative strategy. A zero-shoot production technique proven with one brand applies to the next.

The real disadvantage is divided attention. Your brand is one of several accounts. Response times, strategic depth, and creative ambition can suffer when the agency is stretched. You also have less control over the day-to-day creative process.

Freelancer: Low Cost, Low Ceiling

Freelancers offer the lowest entry point. You can hire an AI-savvy editor for $2-5K/month or pay per deliverable.

Realistic monthly cost: $2-8K depending on volume and skill level.

The real advantage is flexibility. Need 10 ads for a product launch? Hire a freelancer for two weeks. No long-term commitment, no overhead.

The real disadvantage is that freelancers are individuals, not systems. They do not bring testing methodology, performance analysis, or production infrastructure. When a freelancer delivers 15 video ads, you get 15 video ads. When an agency delivered 48 videos for DateMyAge, the output came from 24 strategically written scripts across 22 creators — a systematic approach designed to maximize testing surface area. A freelancer does not think in systems because they are not structured to.

Freelancers also create single points of failure. If your freelancer gets sick, takes another project, or raises rates, your production stops.

When Each Model Wins

Go In-House When:

  • Ad spend exceeds $200K/month and creative is clearly the bottleneck
  • Your category requires deep proprietary knowledge — regulated industries, complex B2B products, or highly technical categories where briefing an external team repeatedly is itself a cost
  • You need 100+ ads per month consistently for 12+ months — the volume justifies the fixed cost
  • You already have 1-2 strong creatives who can anchor the team — building from scratch is much harder than scaling an existing core
  • Creative is a core competitive advantage and you cannot afford to share strategic insights with an agency that might serve adjacent brands

Some of the most successful DTC brands run internal creative teams of 5-10 people producing hundreds of ads monthly. At that scale, the per-unit economics favor in-house decisively.

Go Agency When:

  • Ad spend is $30-200K/month — high enough to need volume, not high enough to justify a full team
  • You need to scale quickly — an agency’s existing systems let you go from zero to 50+ ads per month in weeks, not months
  • You value testing infrastructure — good agencies bring structured creative testing methodology that would take months to develop internally
  • You want cross-category insights — agencies see what works across industries and apply those patterns to your brand
  • Your needs fluctuate — seasonal brands or those with variable launch schedules benefit from flexible capacity

Consider what agencies can do with existing assets. One agency produced 80 fresh ads from old creator footage across 5 brands — zero new shoots required. Another created 60+ ad variations for ZenToes’ Walmart launch from existing footage, delivering 11 videos and 14 static ads. That kind of asset leverage requires systems that a freelancer simply does not have. For more on this approach, see our breakdown of how to scale ad campaigns while maintaining ROAS.

Go Freelancer When:

  • Monthly ad spend is under $30K and you need a handful of new creatives each month
  • You have a specific, well-defined project — a product launch, a seasonal campaign, a one-time content batch
  • You already have creative strategy in-house and just need execution
  • Budget is genuinely constrained and you need to start somewhere before scaling

Freelancers are also excellent for augmenting an existing team. If your in-house team handles strategy and core creative but needs overflow capacity for variations, freelancers fill that gap well.

The Hidden Costs Nobody Mentions

In-House Hidden Costs

  • Recruitment time and cost. Finding an editor who understands both AI tools and performance marketing is harder than it sounds. Expect 2-3 months per hire.
  • Tool sprawl. AI tools evolve fast. Your team will need to continuously evaluate, learn, and switch tools. That costs productivity.
  • Management overhead. Someone has to manage the creative team, review output, maintain quality standards, and connect creative performance data back to production decisions.
  • Opportunity cost of the learning curve. While your team is figuring out workflows, an agency would already be producing and testing. For brands in competitive categories, those lost months matter.

Agency Hidden Costs

  • Communication overhead. Briefing an external team takes time. Feedback loops add days. Revisions require context that an internal team would already have.
  • Onboarding period. Even good agencies need 2-4 weeks to understand your brand, audience, and performance benchmarks.
  • Potential misalignment. The agency’s incentive is often volume. Your incentive is performance. These usually align, but not always.

Freelancer Hidden Costs

  • Quality inconsistency. Without a production system, output quality varies from project to project.
  • Management burden on you. You become the production manager — briefing, reviewing, coordinating, ensuring brand consistency. That time has a cost.
  • No institutional memory. When a freelancer finishes a project, the learnings leave with them. The next freelancer starts from scratch.

The Hybrid Approach

Many brands eventually arrive at a hybrid model, and for good reason. The most common configuration:

Agency for production volume + testing infrastructure. In-house for strategy + brand direction.

This gives you the agency’s production system and cross-category learning while keeping strategic control internal. The brand team writes briefs, approves concepts, and analyzes performance. The agency handles execution, variation, and optimization.

A more advanced hybrid adds freelancers for specialized work — a particular animation style, a niche creator network, or platform-specific content that does not justify agency scope.

A Decision Flowchart

Answer these four questions:

1. What is your monthly ad spend?

  • Under $30K: Start with freelancers
  • $30-200K: Agency is likely optimal
  • Over $200K: In-house becomes viable

2. How quickly do you need to scale?

  • Within weeks: Agency (pre-built systems)
  • Within months: In-house is an option
  • One-off project: Freelancer

3. Do you have creative leadership in place?

  • Yes, strong creative director: In-house team or freelancers under their direction
  • No: Agency provides strategic direction alongside production

4. Is creative volume your primary bottleneck?

  • Yes, consistently: Agency or in-house depending on spend
  • Sometimes: Hybrid model or freelancers for surge capacity
  • Rarely: Freelancers on a project basis

If you answered “agency” to two or more questions, that is probably your starting point. The same logic applies to the other options.

What Good Looks Like in Each Model

Regardless of which model you choose, here is what high-performing AI creative production looks like:

  • Volume with variation. Not 50 identical ads with different thumbnails — 50 genuinely different approaches to the same product. Different hooks, different structures, different emotional angles.
  • Systematic testing. Every batch of creative should test specific hypotheses. Which hooks stop the scroll? Which product angles convert? Which formats work on which platforms?
  • Asset leverage. One shoot or one footage library should produce dozens of outputs, not a handful. When Dadada Baby needed lifestyle images across 6 sales channels, AI-generated visuals delivered 40+ images for $700 — versus $3-8K for a traditional shoot. That kind of asset multiplication should be the standard, not the exception.
  • Performance feedback loops. Creative production must connect to performance data. What is the cost per acquisition? Which creative concepts outperform? That data should inform the next production cycle.

Making the Transition

Some brands start with one model and shift to another as they grow. Common transitions:

Freelancer to agency: When you outgrow 20-30 ads per month and realize you need systems, not just more hands. The agency inherits what the freelancer learned and adds structure.

Agency to in-house: When volume justifies fixed costs and you have identified the right people to hire. Smart brands bring the agency’s methodology in-house rather than starting from scratch. Some keep the agency for overflow or specialized work.

Agency to hybrid: The most natural evolution. The brand hires a creative strategist and 1-2 editors while keeping the agency for production scale. Over time, in-house capacity grows and agency scope narrows to specific functions.

The worst transition is premature in-housing — hiring a team before understanding what workflows they need to run. Brands that work with an agency first learn the production process before bearing the cost of building it. Those that jump straight to in-house pay the learning curve in both dollars and lost time.

The Bottom Line

There is no universally correct answer. The right model depends on your ad spend, your growth stage, your internal capabilities, and your category.

If you are unsure, the lowest-risk starting point for most brands spending $30K+ monthly on ads is an agency with a clear path to hybrid. You get immediate output, you learn the production process, and you can gradually internalize as your needs and team evolve.

The highest-risk move is building in-house without prior experience in AI creative production. The tools change fast, the workflows are non-obvious, and the opportunity cost of a slow ramp-up is real.

Whatever you choose, judge the decision on output quality and testing velocity — not on which model sounds most impressive in a board meeting. The brands winning on paid media in 2026 are the ones testing the most creative variations, learning the fastest, and scaling their winners. The organizational model is just the vehicle.

Questions people ask

Is it cheaper to build an in-house AI creative team or hire an agency?

It depends on volume. In-house teams cost $15-25K/month minimum in salaries and tools before producing a single ad. An agency typically costs $5-15K/month for comparable output. At 100+ ads per month sustained over 12+ months, in-house starts to break even. Below that threshold, an agency is almost always more cost-effective.

Can freelancers handle AI ad creative production at scale?

Individual freelancers rarely scale beyond 20-30 ads per month. They lack the production systems, testing frameworks, and cross-campaign learning that drive performance at volume. Freelancers work well for one-off projects or supplementing an existing team, but not as a primary production engine for brands spending $50K+ monthly on ads.

How long does it take to build an in-house AI creative capability?

Expect 3-6 months to hire, train, and build workflows before reaching consistent output quality. The learning curve includes not just AI tools but also creative testing methodology, performance analysis, and iterative optimization. Most brands underestimate this ramp-up period and the opportunity cost of slower creative output during that time.

What is a hybrid model for AI ad creative production?

A hybrid model uses an agency for high-volume production and testing infrastructure while keeping strategic creative direction in-house. The brand controls messaging and approvals; the agency handles execution, variation, and optimization. This is increasingly common among brands spending $100K-500K monthly on paid media.

When should a brand switch from an agency to in-house AI creative?

Consider the switch when you consistently need 100+ ads per month, have ad spend above $200K/month, can commit to a team of at least 3-4 specialists, and your category requires deep proprietary knowledge that is hard to transfer. Even then, many brands keep an agency for overflow capacity and fresh creative perspectives.
