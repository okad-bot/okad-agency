Faceless UGC Examples: 12 Formats That Get Millions of Views

12 proven faceless UGC formats with real examples — from POV drawing (1.5M views) to ASMR unboxings. Each format explained with when and why to use it.

Guide6 min readby the OKAD team

Faceless UGC is everywhere on TikTok, Reels, and Shorts — but most brands still default to talking-head creator content. That is a missed opportunity. Faceless formats dominate in specific categories and often outperform face-to-camera content on cost-per-view and retention.

Here are 12 faceless UGC formats that work, with real examples of each.

1. POV Process Videos

What it is: First-person perspective of hands performing an action — drawing, cooking, assembling, applying a product. The camera shows what the viewer would see if they were doing it.

Why it works: POV creates immersion. The viewer mentally places themselves in the creator’s position. This drives higher watch time because the brain processes it as an experience, not an advertisement.

Real example: For Skribly (a drawing app), OKAD produced 1,257 POV drawing videos — a hand, a pencil, a voiceover. The best-performing variation: two people drawing together in real time with unexpected outcomes. Result: 1.5M views and 2,000 app registrations. Read the full Skribly case study for the complete production strategy.

Best for: Art and drawing apps, cooking and recipe brands, craft supplies, beauty application, assembly products.

2. Multiplayer / Reaction POV

What it is: Two or more sets of hands interacting — drawing together, cooking the same recipe side by side, competing at a task. The “reaction” element creates tension and resolution.

Why it works: Multiplayer formats add an unpredictability element that single-POV videos lack. The viewer stays to see what happens next. Skribly’s multiplayer drawing format was the single highest-performing variation across all 1,257 videos.

Best for: Apps with social features, competitive products, side-by-side comparisons, couple or friend dynamics.

3. Screen Recording Walkthrough

What it is: Screen capture of an app, website, or software with voiceover narration or text overlays explaining the process.

Why it works: For digital products, the screen IS the product. A talking head explaining an app feature is less convincing than watching the feature work in real time. Screen recordings also double as documentation and onboarding content.

Best for: SaaS products, mobile apps, browser extensions, digital tools, online platforms.

4. Product ASMR

What it is: Close-up footage emphasizing textures, sounds, and visual satisfaction. Tapping on packaging, slicing food, pouring liquids, peeling protective film. Minimal or zero narration.

Why it works: ASMR triggers a sensory response that holds attention. These videos consistently have above-average watch-through rates because the viewer is engaged at a level deeper than information processing.

Best for: Food and beverage, beauty packaging, satisfying textures, premium products with notable tactile qualities.

5. Text-Over-B-Roll

What it is: Short clips of the product in context (being used, sitting on a desk, in a lifestyle setting) with text overlays telling a story — “3 things I wish I knew before…” or “This replaced my entire morning routine.”

Why it works: Text-over-B-roll is the easiest faceless format to produce at scale. The B-roll footage can be reused across multiple videos with different text narratives. It also works with sound off, which matters — 80% of social feeds are browsed on mute.

Best for: Any product category. Particularly effective for DTC brands, supplements, lifestyle products.

6. Before / After Transformation

What it is: Sequential or split-screen footage showing a transformation — messy to clean, raw to cooked, plain to designed, before app to after app.

Why it works: The human brain is wired to notice contrast. Before/after creates an instant value proposition without a single word of sales copy. The transformation IS the pitch.

Best for: Cleaning products, beauty and skincare, design tools, home improvement, fitness supplements, photo/video editing apps.

7. Overhead Flat-Lay Demo

What it is: Top-down camera angle showing hands arranging, demonstrating, or creating something on a surface. Common in recipe videos, product comparisons, and organizational content.

Why it works: The overhead angle gives a clear, unobstructed view of the product and process. It is inherently satisfying to watch from this perspective — Instagram and Pinterest trained audiences to find flat-lay compositions appealing.

Best for: Food preparation, product unboxing, organizational tools, stationery, makeup application, craft projects.

8. Timelapse Creation

What it is: Sped-up footage of a creation process — painting, building, assembling, growing, designing. Often 10–30 seconds compressed from 10–60 minutes of work.

Why it works: Timelapse satisfies curiosity about the full process while respecting the viewer’s time. The compression creates an almost magical quality that drives shares and saves.

Best for: Art supplies, construction/assembly products, gardening, design tools, 3D printing, any product with a visible creation process.

9. Unboxing (Hands Only)

What it is: Hands opening packaging, revealing the product, examining contents. No face — the product is the star.

Why it works: Unboxing taps into the same dopamine response as receiving a gift. Faceless unboxing keeps focus entirely on the product and packaging quality rather than splitting attention with a creator’s reactions.

Best for: Subscription boxes, premium packaging, electronics, beauty products, collector items.

10. Day-in-the-Life (Object Focus)

What it is: Following a product through a typical day — the coffee maker brewing in the morning, the planner being written in at lunch, the skincare routine at night. The “character” is the product, not a person.

Why it works: Lifestyle context makes the product relatable without requiring a relatable person. The viewer inserts themselves into the routine.

Best for: Daily-use products, productivity tools, wellness products, kitchen appliances.

11. Stop Motion

What it is: Frame-by-frame animation of products, ingredients, or components assembling themselves. More production-intensive than other formats but highly distinctive.

Why it works: Stop motion stands out because so few brands invest in it. The format signals creativity and premium positioning. Videos get saved and shared because they feel crafted, not churned out.

Best for: Food brands, premium products, playful or creative brand identities, holiday campaigns.

12. Data / Stats Overlay

What it is: Product footage or lifestyle B-roll with data points, statistics, or customer testimonials appearing as animated text overlays.

Why it works: Combines social proof with visual product content. The data does the selling while the visuals do the branding. Works on mute and is quick to produce variations for testing.

Best for: SaaS metrics, e-commerce conversion stats, supplement clinical data, customer review highlights.

Volume Strategy

The Skribly case proves that faceless content works best at volume. 1,257 videos across 4 accounts and 3 platforms — that volume is only possible because faceless formats are cheap and fast to produce.

The playbook:

  1. Pick 3–4 formats from this list that match your product
  2. Produce 20–50 variations in the first month
  3. Measure hook rate, watch-through, and conversion per format
  4. Kill underperformers, double production on winners
  5. Scale to 100+ videos/month on the winning formats

Faceless UGC is not about avoiding the camera. It is about choosing the format where the product — not the presenter — drives the viewer’s decision. Questions people ask

What are the most popular faceless UGC formats?

The top-performing faceless UGC formats are POV process videos (drawing, cooking, crafting), screen recording walkthroughs, product ASMR, text-over-B-roll storytelling, before/after transformations, overhead flat-lay demos, and timelapse creation videos. Each works best for specific product categories.

Can faceless UGC go viral?

Yes. Faceless content regularly outperforms face-to-camera content on TikTok and Reels. OKAD's Skribly campaign generated 1.5M views with faceless POV drawing content. Cooking, art, ASMR, and satisfying content categories consistently produce viral faceless videos.

Do I need expensive equipment for faceless UGC?

No. A smartphone, a basic tripod or phone mount, and good lighting are enough. For POV formats, a chest-mounted or overhead phone holder costs $15–$30. Screen recording requires only screen capture software. The constraint is creative direction, not equipment.

Which industries use faceless UGC the most?

Food and beverage, beauty and skincare, mobile apps, arts and crafts, cleaning products, tech and SaaS, and educational content. Any industry where the product or process is more compelling than a person talking about it.
