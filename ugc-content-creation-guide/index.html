01 — Overview

What UGC content creation actually involves

  • UGC content creation is a structured production process, not casual social media posting
  • The workflow has seven stages: brief, research, script, setup, film, edit, deliver
  • Creators who follow a repeatable process deliver faster and get rehired more often

UGC content creation is the process of producing authentic, creator-driven video content for brands. Unlike influencer marketing, where the creator posts on their own account, UGC creators deliver raw or edited video files that brands use in their own ads, social posts, and product pages. The creator is a content producer, not a media channel.

The workflow spans seven distinct stages: receiving and interpreting the brief, researching the brand and audience, writing the script, setting up equipment and environment, filming the content, editing the footage, and delivering the final assets. Each stage has specific techniques and standards that separate amateur content from professional-grade deliverables.

What makes this process different from creating personal content is the accountability. A brand is paying for specific deliverables to a specific standard by a specific deadline. There is no room for “I’ll figure it out when I start filming.” The most successful creators in OKAD’s 700+ network treat every project like a mini production — planned, executed, and delivered with a repeatable system that guarantees consistent quality.

This guide walks through every stage of that system. Whether you are filming your first UGC video or refining a workflow you have used for months, the frameworks here are drawn from what OKAD’s top-performing creators do in practice — not theory. The goal is to give you a complete production process you can follow from the moment a brief lands in your inbox to the moment you hit send on the final delivery.

02 — Understanding the Brief

How to read and interpret brand briefs

  • Six key elements: product, audience, tone, format, deliverables, timeline
  • Ask clarifying questions before you start — never assume what a vague brief means
  • Create a personal checklist to verify you have everything before filming

Every UGC project starts with a brief. Some briefs are detailed documents with reference videos and exact scripts. Others are a single paragraph in a DM. Regardless of format, your job is to extract six essential elements before you do anything else: the product (what you are featuring and its key selling points), the audience (who the video is trying to reach), the tone (casual, energetic, educational, aspirational), the format (testimonial, unboxing, tutorial, lifestyle), the deliverables (number of videos, aspect ratio, duration, hook variations), and the timeline (when raw footage and final edits are due).

If any of these six elements are missing from the brief, ask. This is the single most important habit that separates professional creators from beginners. Beginners receive a vague brief and start filming immediately, hoping they will figure it out. Professionals send a short list of clarifying questions within 24 hours. Questions like: “Should the video focus on taste or convenience?” or “Do you want captions baked in, or will your team add them?” These take two minutes to write and prevent days of wasted work.

Pay special attention to the dos and don’ts section. This is where brands list competitor names you cannot mention, health or financial claims you cannot make, specific phrases they require, and visual restrictions. Missing a compliance requirement does not just trigger a reshoot — it can create legal liability. If the brief does not include a dos and don’ts section, ask for one. Every brand has restrictions, even if they have not written them down yet.

Build a personal brief checklist — a simple document listing every piece of information you need before filming. Run every new brief through this checklist. If boxes are empty, message the brand. OKAD uses a standardized brief template across all projects for exactly this reason: it forces completeness and eliminates the guessing game.

03 — Research Phase

Studying the brand before you create

  • Spend 20–30 minutes researching the brand, competitors, and audience before scripting
  • Study existing UGC from the brand’s ads library — it reveals what they have already tested
  • Review product reviews and Reddit threads for authentic language and real pain points

The research phase is the most skipped step in UGC production, and it is the reason most first drafts miss the mark. Spending 20–30 minutes understanding the brand, its competitors, and its audience before you write a single word of script dramatically increases the odds that your content resonates on the first take.

Start with the brand itself. Visit their website, browse their social profiles, and read their product descriptions. Note the language they use — is it clinical and precise, or casual and playful? Check their existing ads in the Meta Ad Library and TikTok Creative Center. These platforms show you exactly what the brand has already tested, which means you can avoid duplicating existing creative and identify angles they have not explored yet.

Next, study the competition. Search for 3–5 competitors in the same category and look at their UGC ads. What hooks are they using? What claims are they making? This gives you context for differentiation. If every competitor leads with a price comparison hook, your brand might benefit from an emotional hook instead. If the category is saturated with testimonials, a tutorial format might stand out.

Finally, research the target audience. Read Amazon reviews, Reddit threads, and TikTok comments about the product or category. Pay attention to the exact words real customers use to describe their problems and results. These phrases are gold for scripting because they sound authentic — they are authentic. A creator who says “I was tired of my foundation oxidizing by noon” sounds more real than one who says “This product provides long-lasting coverage” because the first phrase is how actual people talk.

04 — Scripting

Hook formulas, body structure, and CTA approaches

  • Every UGC script follows three parts: hook (0–3s), body (3–45s), CTA (last 5–10s)
  • Write 3–5 hook variations per video — the hook determines hook rate and ad performance
  • Script bullet points, not word-for-word dialogue — authenticity dies when you read a teleprompter

Scripting is where your research becomes a production plan. A UGC script is not a Hollywood screenplay — it is a structured outline that ensures your video hits every key message while leaving room for natural delivery. The best scripts are written as bullet points with key phrases, not word-for-word monologues. This preserves the authenticity that makes UGC outperform studio-produced ads.

The hook is the first 1–3 seconds and determines whether anyone sees the rest of your video. The hook rate — the percentage of viewers who watch past the opening — is the single most important metric in paid UGC. Three hook formulas consistently perform: the question hook (“Why does nobody talk about this?”), the bold claim hook (“This $15 serum replaced my entire skincare routine”), and the problem-agitation hook (“I used to spend an hour on my hair every morning”). Write 3–5 variations using different formulas for each video concept.

The body covers the core message in 20–40 seconds. Structure it around one of three frameworks: problem-solution (describe the frustration, introduce the product as the fix), before-after (show the old way vs. the new way with the product), or feature-benefit (highlight 2–3 specific features and why each matters to the viewer). Do not try to cover every product feature — pick the 1–3 most relevant to the target audience and go deep on those.

The CTA should be direct and specific. “Link in bio” is the default for organic posts. “Check it out at [brand].com” works for ads. “I’ll leave the link below” is natural for talking-head formats. Avoid stacking multiple CTAs — one clear instruction converts better than three competing ones. Match the CTA energy to the rest of the video: if the tone is casual and conversational, a hard-sell CTA will feel jarring.

Before filming, read your script bullet points out loud. If any phrase sounds like ad copy rather than something you would say to a friend, rewrite it. The entire value of UGC is that it feels like a real person sharing a real opinion. That feeling starts in the script, not on camera.

05 — Pre-Production Setup

Equipment checklist, lighting, and environment

  • A smartphone, a tripod, and natural window light are enough to start
  • Clean your background — clutter kills the professional feel of UGC content
  • Audio quality matters more than video quality — invest in a lavalier mic before a ring light

Pre-production is the 15–20 minutes of setup that prevents hours of reshooting. The goal is not a studio-quality set — it is a clean, well-lit, distraction-free environment where the product and your delivery are the focus. Most UGC is shot on a smartphone, and that is exactly what brands want. The tools are deliberately simple.

Equipment checklist

  • Smartphone — iPhone 12+ or equivalent Android. Shoot in 4K at 30fps if your phone supports it. 1080p is acceptable for most deliverables.
  • Tripod or phone mount — $15–30 is sufficient. Shaky handheld footage is fine for some formats (GRWM, vlog) but a tripod gives you consistent framing for talking head and product shots.
  • Lavalier microphone — $20–50 wired or wireless clip-on mic. Audio quality is the number one technical factor viewers notice. Bad audio makes professional video look amateur.
  • Lighting — Natural window light is the best free option. Face the window, never have it behind you. For evening or overcast days, a $30–60 LED panel provides consistent, flattering light.
  • Product — Unbox and inspect the product before filming. Know how to open it, use it, and display it. Fumbling with packaging on camera wastes takes.

Environment setup

Background. Remove clutter, personal items, and anything that distracts from the product. A clean kitchen counter, a tidy bathroom shelf, or a neutral wall with one decorative item works for 90% of UGC formats. Match the setting to the product category: skincare belongs in a bathroom, food products belong in a kitchen, tech belongs at a desk.

Wardrobe. Unless the brief specifies otherwise, wear what you would normally wear. Authenticity is the goal. Avoid logos from other brands, busy patterns that create visual noise, and pure white or black (both cause exposure problems on camera). Solid, muted colors in the mid-range work best.

Test shot. Before you start your actual takes, record a 10-second test clip. Check framing, lighting, audio levels, and background. Play it back with sound. Fix any issues now — not after you have filmed eight takes of the full script.

06 — Filming Techniques

Framing, angles, multiple takes, and b-roll

  • Film in 9:16 vertical for TikTok, Reels, and Shorts — confirm with the brief for any other ratios
  • Record 3–5 complete takes minimum — your best take is rarely your first
  • Capture b-roll of the product from multiple angles for editing flexibility

Framing. For talking-head content, position yourself in the center or slightly off-center of the frame with your eyes roughly at the top third of the screen. Leave a small gap above your head — too much headroom looks awkward, too little feels claustrophobic. Keep the camera at eye level or slightly above. Below eye level (looking up at you) creates an unflattering angle that brands rarely want.

Camera angles. Vary your angles within a single video to maintain visual interest. A standard UGC video might combine a medium talking-head shot (chest up), a close-up product shot (filling 70% of the frame), and an over-the-shoulder angle showing the product in use. Changing angles every 5–8 seconds prevents the viewer’s eye from getting bored — this is especially important for videos longer than 30 seconds.

Multiple takes. Record at least 3–5 complete takes of the full script. Your first take is a warm-up — your delivery will be tighter, more natural, and more confident by the third. Some creators film 8–10 takes and select the best segments from each in editing. This approach takes more filming time but produces a noticeably better final product because you can cherry-pick the best delivery of each section.

B-roll. After finishing your talking-head takes, spend 5–10 minutes capturing b-roll of the product. Film the product from multiple angles: overhead flat lay, side profile, detail shots of textures or labels, the product in use (applying, pouring, clicking, opening). B-roll gives you editing flexibility — you can cover jump cuts, illustrate claims, and add visual variety without reshooting the entire video.

Audio. Record in a quiet environment. Turn off fans, close windows, and silence your phone notifications. If you are using a lavalier mic, do a clap sync at the start of each take so you can align the audio in post. Speak at a consistent volume — avoid the common mistake of starting quietly and getting louder as you warm up. If you stumble on a line, pause for two seconds and restart that sentence from the beginning rather than stopping the entire take.

07 — Editing Workflow

Software, pacing, music, captions, and exports

  • CapCut for speed, Premiere Pro for control, DaVinci Resolve for free professional power
  • Cut ruthlessly — every second that does not serve the message should be removed
  • Captions are mandatory — 85% of social video is watched without sound

Editing transforms raw footage into a deliverable. The goal is not cinematic production — it is a tight, watchable video that holds attention and delivers the brand’s message. For most UGC, editing means: selecting the best take, cutting dead air, adding captions, inserting b-roll over transitions, and exporting in the correct format. That is it. Over-editing is as much of a problem as under-editing.

Editing principles for UGC

Pacing. Cut every pause longer than half a second. Remove filler words (“um,” “like,” “so basically”). The resulting jump cuts are not just acceptable in UGC — they are expected. Fast pacing signals energy and confidence. A 60-second raw take typically becomes a 30–40 second edited video once you remove dead air.

Captions. Add captions to every video. An estimated 85% of social media video is watched without sound. CapCut’s auto-caption feature handles this in minutes. Style your captions to be readable: bold font, high contrast against the video, placed in the center-lower third of the frame. Avoid the top and bottom edges where platform UI elements overlap.

Music. Use royalty-free background music or trending sounds as specified in the brief. Music should be at 10–15% volume relative to your voice — barely noticeable but filling the silence between sentences. Never use copyrighted music unless the brand explicitly provides it or clears the rights.

Export settings. Deliver in the format the brief specifies. Standard defaults: 9:16 aspect ratio, 1080x1920 resolution minimum, H.264 codec, 30fps. Export at the highest quality your editor supports. If the brand needs multiple ratios (9:16 + 1:1 + 16:9), create separate exports for each — do not just crop the same video, as framing needs to be adjusted per ratio.

08 — Delivery & Communication

File naming, delivery format, and revision etiquette

  • Professional file naming saves brands 30+ minutes of organizing per delivery batch
  • Deliver via Google Drive or WeTransfer — never through DMs or compressed messaging apps
  • Handle revisions professionally — your response to feedback determines whether you get rehired

File naming. Use a consistent naming convention that identifies the content at a glance: [Brand]_[Format]_[HookVariant]_[CreatorName]_[Date]. For example: Acme_Testimonial_Hook1_JaneDoe_Sep2026.mp4. This may seem like a small detail, but brands managing 50+ creator deliveries per month notice immediately when files arrive named final_video_2.mp4 versus properly labeled. Clean file naming signals professionalism and makes the brand’s production manager’s job easier — which means they think of you first for the next project.

Delivery format. Upload to Google Drive or WeTransfer and share the link via email. Never deliver final content through Instagram DMs, WhatsApp, or iMessage — these platforms compress video quality. Google Drive preserves the original file quality and gives both parties a permanent, organized record of the delivery. Create a folder structure: Brand Name / Delivery Date / Videos. Include a brief text summary listing each deliverable and its specs.

Revision etiquette. Revisions are normal, not a personal critique. When you receive feedback, acknowledge it within 24 hours, confirm you understand the requested changes, and provide a revised delivery timeline. Do not defend your creative choices or explain why you did it that way — just make the changes. The standard is 1–2 revision rounds included in the project fee. If a brand requests changes beyond the agreed scope, politely reference the contract and propose a fair additional fee for the extra work.

Professional communication. Respond to brand messages within 24 hours on business days. Send proactive updates when filming or editing takes longer than expected — do not go silent. After delivering the final files, send a brief follow-up: “All files delivered. Let me know if you need any adjustments. I enjoyed working on this project and would be happy to collaborate again.” This single message plants the seed for repeat business, which is where the real money in UGC lives.

09 — Common Mistakes

Top 10 mistakes new UGC creators make

  • Every mistake on this list is fixable — most cost nothing but awareness
  • The top three mistakes account for 70% of revision requests across OKAD’s network
  • Avoiding these puts you ahead of 80% of creators applying for the same gigs
  1. Filming before reading the brief carefully. Skimming the brief and starting immediately is the fastest path to reshoots. Read it twice, extract the six key elements, ask questions, then film.
  2. Bad audio. Viewers tolerate imperfect video but abandon videos with muffled, echoey, or quiet audio within seconds. A $20 lavalier mic solves this permanently.
  3. Only filming one take. Your first take is always your worst. Film at least 3–5 complete takes and select the best segments. The difference in delivery quality between take one and take four is dramatic.
  4. Over-editing. Adding transitions, filters, stickers, and effects dilutes the authentic feel that makes UGC work. Brands are paying for content that looks real. Keep editing minimal: cuts, captions, and subtle background music.
  5. Ignoring the safe zones. Text and key visual elements need to avoid the top and bottom 15% of the frame where platform UI (profile icon, captions bar, like buttons) will overlay. Missing this means your captions or product name gets hidden.
  6. No b-roll. Talking-head-only videos feel flat after 15 seconds. B-roll of the product, your hands using it, or close-up texture shots break up the monotony and illustrate your claims visually.
  7. Sounding scripted. Reading word-for-word from a script kills authenticity. Use bullet points with key phrases and deliver them conversationally. If it sounds like you are reading, you are reading.
  8. Delivering compressed files. Sending videos through WhatsApp or Instagram DMs compresses the quality. Always deliver through Google Drive or WeTransfer to preserve the full resolution.
  9. Not naming files properly. video_final_v3_REAL_FINAL.mp4 is not a file name. Use the brand-format-hook-name-date convention. Brands notice this detail more than you think.
  10. Taking revision feedback personally. Revisions are part of the process, not a judgment on your talent. Creators who handle feedback gracefully and deliver revisions quickly are the ones brands rehire.
10 — FAQ

Frequently asked questions

For an experienced creator, the full process takes 2 to 4 hours per video: 20 minutes reading the brief and researching, 20 minutes scripting, 15 minutes setting up, 30 to 60 minutes filming multiple takes, 30 to 60 minutes editing, and 10 minutes organizing and delivering files. New creators should expect this to take closer to 6 to 8 hours until they develop a repeatable workflow.
A smartphone (iPhone 12 or newer, or equivalent Android), a basic tripod or phone mount ($15 to $30), and a clip-on lavalier microphone ($20 to $50). Natural window light is free and sufficient for most shooting situations. You do not need a ring light, a DSLR camera, or a professional studio. Brands want content that looks authentic, not cinematic. Upgrade your equipment only when specific client requests demand it.
Check the brief — it should specify. Some brands want fully edited videos with captions and music. Others want raw footage so their in-house team can edit. If the brief does not specify, ask. Delivering edited content when a brand wanted raw footage wastes your editing time. Delivering raw footage when they expected a polished final creates the impression that you did not finish the job.
Three to five hook variations per video concept is the industry standard. Each hook should use a different approach: a question, a bold claim, a problem statement. Hooks are the highest-leverage element for ad performance, so brands test multiple hooks against the same body and CTA. Filming 5 hooks adds only 10 to 15 minutes to your shooting session but significantly increases the value of your delivery.

DaVinci Resolve is the best free alternative for desktop editing when you need more control over color, audio, or multi-track editing. Adobe Premiere Pro is worth the subscription if you are producing high-volume content and need batch processing and advanced export controls.
Refer to your contract. However, some enterprise contracts restrict this. Always clarify portfolio usage rights before signing. If the contract is silent on the topic, ask explicitly. Having strong portfolio pieces is essential for landing future work, so this clause matters.
