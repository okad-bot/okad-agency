The script is the ad. Not the lighting, not the creator's face, not the editing. Before anyone opens a camera, the script determines whether the ad will stop a thumb or get scrolled past.

Most brands write UGC scripts like mini commercials: product intro, feature list, call to action. These scripts produce content that looks like UGC but performs like a banner ad nobody asked for.

The scripts that actually sell follow a different structure. It is not complicated, but it is specific — and the details matter more than most marketers realize.

## The 4-Beat Script Formula

Every high-performing UGC ad follows four beats. The timing varies by platform, but the sequence does not:

**Beat 1: Hook (0–3 seconds)**
Stop the scroll. This is the only job. If the hook fails, nothing else matters. Your [hook rate](/blog/blog-hook-rate-explained/) — the percentage of viewers who watch past 3 seconds — is the single most predictive metric for ad performance.

**Beat 2: Problem or Context (3–10 seconds)**
Establish why the viewer should care. This is not about the product yet. It is about the viewer's world — their frustration, their goal, their situation.

**Beat 3: Solution and Demo (10–25 seconds)**
Introduce the product as the answer to Beat 2. Show it in action. Be specific: numbers, timeframes, visible results. Vague claims kill credibility.

**Beat 4: CTA (last 3–5 seconds)**
Tell them exactly what to do next. One action, one reason to act now.

When we scripted [24 ad scripts for DateMyAge](/cases/datemyage-dating-app-scripts/) — producing 48 videos across 22 creators — every script followed this 4-beat structure. The variation came from which hook angle and body format we chose, not from reinventing the structure each time.

## 15 Hook Formulas That Actually Work

The hook is where scripts succeed or fail. Here are 15 formulas tested across hundreds of campaigns:

### Direct Problem Hooks
1. **"I was struggling with [problem] until..."** — Relatable pain point, implies a solution exists
2. **"Stop doing [common mistake] with your [product category]"** — Pattern interrupt, positions viewer as making an error
3. **"Nobody tells you this about [category]..."** — Information gap, promises insider knowledge

### Curiosity Hooks
4. **"I tested [product] for [time period]. Here is what happened."** — Curiosity plus implicit review
5. **"The reason your [routine/approach] is not working"** — Challenges an assumption
6. **"I found [unexpected thing] and I need to tell someone"** — Discovery energy, urgency

### Social Proof Hooks
7. **"[Number] people switched to [product] last month"** — Bandwagon effect with specificity
8. **"My [friend/dermatologist/trainer] told me about this"** — Third-party credibility
9. **"This has [number] five-star reviews and I finally tried it"** — Review validation plus personal test

### Controversial Take Hooks
10. **"Unpopular opinion: [bold statement about category]"** — Triggers engagement through disagreement
11. **"I do not care what anyone says, [product] is [bold claim]"** — Emotional commitment signals authenticity
12. **"[Expensive alternative] is a waste of money. Here is why."** — Comparison creates instant stakes

### Before/After Hooks
13. **"Watch what happens when I use [product] for the first time"** — Real-time transformation promise
14. **"Day 1 vs Day 30 with [product]"** — Time-lapse credibility
15. **"I replaced [old solution] with [product]. The difference is wild."** — Direct comparison with emotional payoff

Not every hook works for every product. Test 3–5 hook variations per script body. For the [longevity supplement campaign](/cases/longevity-supplement-story-ads/) that produced 68 ads achieving EUR 183 CPA, story-format hooks (formulas 4 and 14) consistently outperformed direct problem hooks — because the audience responded to personal transformation narratives over pain-point messaging.

## 5 Body Formats

Once the hook lands, the body carries the viewer to the CTA. Choose the format that matches your product and audience:

### 1. Testimonial
The creator shares their personal experience. Works best for products with visible results (skincare, fitness, before/after transformations). Structure: what I tried before, what changed, specific result.

### 2. Tutorial / How-To
The creator demonstrates how to use the product. Works for anything with a usage learning curve — apps, multi-step products, tools. Structure: step 1, step 2, step 3, result.

### 3. Comparison
The creator compares the product to an alternative. Works when you have a clear advantage over a known competitor or category. Structure: old way vs new way, specific difference, proof point.

### 4. Story
The creator tells a narrative arc: problem, discovery, transformation. The most emotionally engaging format but also the longest — typically 30–60 seconds. Our [dating app content pipeline](/cases/your-christian-date-broll-content/) producing 60 videos per month relied heavily on story-format scripts because personal narratives drove the highest engagement in the dating vertical.

### 5. Challenge / Experiment
The creator tests a claim or tries something for the first time on camera. Works when your product's benefit is demonstrable in real time. Structure: "I am going to try [claim]," demonstrate, react to result.

## CTA Formulas

The CTA is the shortest beat but the one that converts. Four formulas:

**Urgency:** "Link in bio — this deal ends [timeframe]." Simple, direct, creates time pressure.

**Social proof:** "Join [number] people who already [benefit]. Link below." Reinforces that others have acted.

**Risk reversal:** "Try it for [period] — if you do not love it, [guarantee]." Removes the objection.

**Benefit restatement:** "If you want [specific result from Beat 3], tap the link." Connects the CTA directly to the demonstrated value.

Use one CTA per ad. Combining multiple ("link in bio, use code X, and follow for more") dilutes the action. One instruction, one reason.

## Script Length by Platform

| Platform | Optimal Length | Word Count | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| TikTok | 15–30 seconds | 60–120 words | Front-load value. Viewer patience is shortest here. |
| Meta Feed | 20–45 seconds | 80–180 words | Can go longer if the hook is strong. Story format works. |
| Meta Stories | 15–25 seconds | 60–100 words | Must resolve within the story card duration. |
| YouTube Shorts | 30–60 seconds | 120–240 words | Audience tolerates longer format. Tutorials perform well. |
| Instagram Reels | 15–30 seconds | 60–120 words | Similar to TikTok. Trending audio can extend watch time. |

These are starting points. Test shorter and longer versions of your winning scripts. Some products need 45 seconds to demonstrate; others sell in 12.

## Scripting for Creators vs. AI Delivery

The script format changes depending on who delivers it:

### For Human Creators

Write a brief, not a teleprompter script. Include:
- **Exact hook** (word-for-word — this is the testable variable)
- **Key talking points** for the body (3–5 bullet points, not full sentences)
- **Exact CTA** (word-for-word)
- **Visual direction** (what to show, where to look, what to hold)
- **Tone reference** (casual, excited, skeptical-turned-convinced, matter-of-fact)

Creators who read a script sound like they are reading a script. Give them structure with room to breathe.

### For AI-Generated UGC

Write word-for-word scripts. AI delivery tools (AI avatars, text-to-speech) need exact text because they cannot improvise or add natural pauses on their own. Include:
- Full script text with punctuation for pacing
- Emphasis markers for key phrases
- Pause indicators between beats
- Pronunciation guides for brand names or technical terms

The trade-off: AI-delivered scripts sound more consistent but less authentic. Use AI delivery for high-volume testing where you need 20+ variations fast. Use human creators for the winning scripts you want to scale.

## How to Batch-Produce Scripts at Scale

Writing one script at a time is slow. Here is the batch process:

**Step 1: Define your angles.** For each product, list 3–5 distinct selling angles (price, results, convenience, ingredient/feature, social proof).

**Step 2: Assign formats.** Match each angle to 1–2 body formats. A results angle works as a testimonial or before/after. A convenience angle works as a tutorial.

**Step 3: Write hooks.** For each angle-format combination, write 3 hook variations using different formulas from the list above.

**Step 4: Assemble full scripts.** Combine hooks + body format + CTA. Each unique combination is a separate script.

**Step 5: Prioritize for production.** Rank scripts by expected performance (based on past data or category benchmarks) and produce the top 15–20 first.

This process generated the script library for our [ad creative strategy pipeline](/blog/blog-ad-creative-strategy/) — where the brief-to-scale lifecycle starts with script batching before any production begins.

## Common Script Mistakes

**Writing features instead of benefits.** "Contains 10mg of hyaluronic acid" is a feature. "My skin stopped flaking after one week" is a benefit. UGC scripts sell benefits.

**Generic hooks.** "This product changed my life" tells the viewer nothing specific. "I stopped spending $200 a month on [alternative] because of this" gives them a reason to keep watching.

**Too many points.** One script, one core message. If you have three selling points, write three scripts — do not cram them all into one.

**No visual direction.** A script that says "talk about the product" gives the creator nothing to work with. "Hold the bottle at eye level, squeeze one pump onto your hand, show the texture" is a script a creator can execute.

**Skipping the problem beat.** Jumping straight from hook to product demo skips the emotional setup. The problem beat is where the viewer thinks "that is me" — without it, the product demo is just a demo.

The script is where ad performance is built or broken. Get the structure right, test the hooks aggressively, and let the data — not your intuition — pick the winners.
