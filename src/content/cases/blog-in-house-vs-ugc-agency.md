The question is not whether UGC works. It does. Brands across e-commerce, SaaS, and consumer apps have proven that creator-fronted content outperforms polished brand ads on cost-per-acquisition.

The real question is how to produce it. Brands choosing between hiring an in-house content person and working with a [UGC agency](/blog/blog-what-is-ugc-agency/) face a classic build-vs-buy decision, and the financial implications are larger than most teams expect going in.

## The True Cost of In-House UGC

Hiring an in-house UGC coordinator looks simple on paper. A salary line, maybe a software budget. In practice, the full cost stack is much higher than the number on the job posting.

A content coordinator salary alone runs $45,000-$65,000 per year in most markets. That is before anything else is added to the picture.

Benefits and overhead -- health insurance, payroll taxes, paid time off, office or remote stipends -- typically add $15,000-$25,000 on top of salary. Software and tools for scripting, editing, asset management, and reporting run $3,000-$6,000 per year. And here is the part teams frequently miss: an in-house coordinator still needs external creators to shoot content, which means ongoing creator payments of $2,000-$5,000 per month, or $24,000-$60,000 annually.

Add equipment -- cameras, lighting, editing hardware -- at $2,000-$5,000 upfront, plus the time a senior marketing lead spends managing and reviewing this person's work. That management time is real cost even when it does not appear on an invoice.

Total first-year cost for a single in-house hire: $100,000-$150,000. And this person, working at full capacity, produces roughly 10-15 videos per month. That puts the effective cost per video well above what most brands assume when they first consider hiring.

## Agency Cost Structure

Agency pricing works differently. Instead of a fixed headcount cost, you pay for output through a monthly retainer, and the retainer scales with volume rather than with hiring decisions.

Typical [UGC agency pricing](/blog/blog-ugc-agency-pricing/) runs $2,500-$10,000 per month. That retainer covers strategy, scripting, creator sourcing, production management, editing, and revisions -- the full production pipeline, not just raw footage.

For 20 videos per month, annual cost lands around $48,000-$72,000. For 40 videos per month, expect $72,000-$120,000 annually. Both figures are comparable to or lower than the cost of a single in-house hire, but they come with no hiring risk, no fixed overhead, and no ramp-up period.

This matters because the agency model absorbs the operational burden that otherwise falls on your team: managing creator relationships, chasing revisions, and keeping a production calendar running week after week.

At both volume levels, the in-house cost per video stays meaningfully higher, mainly because fixed salary and overhead do not scale down even when output is low, and scaling up requires discrete, lumpy hiring decisions rather than a simple retainer adjustment.

## Speed and Scalability

Scaling in-house production is a hiring problem disguised as a production problem. Each additional 10-15 videos of monthly capacity generally requires another hire, and each hire comes with a 2-3 month ramp-up period before they reach full productivity. Going from 15 to 30 videos per month in-house is not a decision you can make this week -- it is a decision you make this quarter.

Scaling with an agency is a conversation about retainer size. Moving from 20 to 50 videos per month typically takes a single call and can be implemented within a week, because the agency already has the creator network, workflows, and production capacity to absorb the increase. For brands needing 20 or more videos per month, this speed advantage is often decisive on its own, independent of the cost comparison.

## Quality and Creative Direction

In-house production has one clear strength: deep, intuitive brand knowledge. A coordinator who lives inside your brand every day understands nuance that an outside partner has to learn. The tradeoff is range -- one person's creative perspective, applied repeatedly, tends to converge on familiar formats and angles over time.

Agencies offer the opposite tradeoff. They bring broader creative expertise drawn from [running high-volume production](/blog/blog-high-volume-ugc-production/) across many brands and industries, access to a wider pool of creator styles, and performance data pulled from dozens of accounts rather than one. That cross-account visibility often surfaces winning formats a single in-house hire would never think to test.

Neither approach is universally better on quality. The difference is depth versus breadth, and which one matters more depends on how much creative range your campaigns actually need.

## The Hybrid Model

At higher volumes, many brands land on a hybrid structure rather than choosing one model exclusively. An in-house creative director sets brand strategy, approves concepts, and maintains consistency, while an agency handles the production itself -- sourcing creators, managing shoots, editing, and delivering finished assets.

This model tends to work well once volume crosses roughly 30 videos per month. The in-house side protects brand voice and strategic direction. The agency side provides the execution speed and creator diversity needed to hit that volume without a large internal team. It is a middle path that avoids both the overhead of a full in-house department and the loss of brand control some teams worry about with full outsourcing.

## Bottom Line

Below 15 videos per month, an agency is clearly more cost-effective. The fixed overhead of an in-house hire simply does not pay for itself at low volume.

Between 15 and 30 videos per month, an agency still wins on total cost and offers more flexibility, since retainers can flex up or down without hiring or layoffs.

Above 30 videos per month, a hybrid model is worth serious consideration -- in-house strategy paired with agency execution. Pure in-house production only makes sense above roughly 50 videos per month, and only with a dedicated team rather than a single hire stretched across every function.

For most brands, the lowest-risk path is starting with an agency, learning what formats and creators actually perform, and building an in-house function later once volume and proven playbooks justify the fixed cost.
