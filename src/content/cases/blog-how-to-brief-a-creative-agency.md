We read over 50 creative briefs a month. Some are two sentences in a DM. Others are 14-page PDFs where the actual request is buried on page nine. Both waste time — ours and yours.

The difference between a project that ships on time with two rounds of revision and one that drags through five rounds and a frustrated Slack thread almost always comes down to the brief. Not talent, not budget, not tools. The brief.

This post breaks down exactly what we need from you to do our best work: the seven sections a creative brief should cover, the mistakes we see most often, and a template you can copy and fill in before your next project.

## Why the Brief Matters More Than You Think

A brief is not a formality. It is the single document that every designer, developer, copywriter, and strategist on the project will reference. When the brief is vague, every person on the team fills the gaps with their own assumptions. Those assumptions diverge. The first draft comes back and you say "that is not what I meant." Of course it is not — because the brief never said what you meant.

A strong brief does three things:

1. **Defines the problem.** Not the solution — the problem. What is broken, what is missing, what opportunity exists.
2. **Sets boundaries.** What is in scope, what is out, what has already been decided, what is still open.
3. **Gives the team a way to evaluate their own work.** If the brief says "reduce bounce rate on mobile," the team can measure their design decisions against that goal. If the brief says "make it modern," nobody knows when they have succeeded.

## The 7 Sections of a Good Creative Brief

These seven sections cover everything an agency needs to start working without a round of clarification emails. You do not need to write a novel for each one — a few sentences per section is often enough.

### 1. Business Context

Tell us who you are, what you sell, and what problem your business solves. We are not asking for your life story. We need enough context to understand why this project matters right now.

Good example: "We sell B2B SaaS for construction project management. We just raised a Series A and need to reposition from 'scrappy startup' to 'enterprise-ready platform.' Our current site looks like it was built in 2019 because it was."

This gives us industry, stage, positioning goal, and urgency. Three sentences.

### 2. Target Audience

Who will see this work? Be specific. "Everyone" is not an audience.

We need to know their role (decision-maker, end user, influencer), their level of familiarity with your product or category, and what matters to them. A landing page for CFOs reads differently than one for junior developers, even if the product is the same.

If you have buyer personas, share them. If you do not, a paragraph describing your best customer works just as well.

### 3. Deliverables

List exactly what you need. Not "a website" — how many pages, which pages, is there a blog, do you need a CMS, are there integrations. Not "some videos" — how many, what length, for which platform, vertical or horizontal, with or without voiceover.

Ambiguity here is the number one cause of scope creep and mismatched expectations. If you are unsure about specifics, say so — "we think 5-7 pages but open to your recommendation" is perfectly fine. What hurts is when you write "a website" and later reveal you also need an e-commerce store with 200 product pages.

Check [our pricing](/pricing) to get a sense of what different deliverables typically cost. It will help you scope your list before writing the brief.

### 4. Brand Guidelines

If you have a brand book, attach it. If you have brand colors, fonts, a logo file, tone of voice guidelines — include all of it.

If you do not have formal guidelines, tell us that. It changes how we approach the project. We may need to establish visual direction first before moving into execution, and that is a different kind of work with a different timeline.

At minimum, we need:

- Logo files (SVG or AI preferred, PNG at minimum)
- Brand colors (hex codes)
- Fonts you use or like
- Tone of voice (formal, casual, technical, playful — pick one or two words)

### 5. References and Anti-References

This is the section most briefs either skip entirely or do badly. References are not "inspiration." They are communication tools that help us understand your taste faster than words can.

**How to do references well:**

- Share 3-5 examples, not 20. We need signal, not noise.
- For each reference, write one sentence about what you like. "I like the typography hierarchy on this site" is useful. "Check this out" is not.
- Include anti-references — things you actively do not want. "We do not want anything that looks like [competitor X] because our customers see us as the premium alternative" tells us more than a mood board.

### 6. Timeline

When does this need to be live? Work backward from there and tell us about any hard deadlines — product launches, trade shows, funding rounds, seasonal campaigns.

Be honest about whether the deadline is real or aspirational. A real deadline (the trade show is November 15, no matter what) changes how we staff and structure the project. An aspirational deadline (we would love to launch by Q1) gives us room to recommend a more thorough process.

If you do not have a timeline, say so. We will propose one based on the scope.

### 7. Budget

State your range. We understand that budgets are sensitive, but hiding the number creates more problems than it solves.

Here is why: if your budget is $5K and we propose a $25K solution, we both wasted a week. If we know the range upfront, we scope the work to fit. A $5K website and a $50K website are both real projects — they just involve different approaches, and we need to know which conversation we are having.

If you genuinely do not know what something should cost, say that and ask us for a ballpark. That is a completely valid starting point. You can [start a project](/#contact) with us to get a quick estimate before writing the full brief.

## Common Mistakes That Waste Revision Rounds

We have seen every version of a bad brief. These five patterns cost you the most time and money.

### 1. Describing the Solution Instead of the Problem

"We want a website with a hero video, three-column feature section, testimonials slider, and a sticky header" is not a brief. It is a wireframe described in words. When you prescribe the solution, you take away the agency's ability to solve the actual problem — and you are paying the agency specifically for that ability.

Instead, describe what is not working: "Visitors land on our homepage and leave within 10 seconds. We think the value proposition is unclear." Now the team can design a solution that actually addresses the issue.

### 2. No References or Only Positive References

When you share five sites you love but nothing you dislike, we have to guess at your boundaries. Maybe you love minimalism but three of your references happen to have bold typography — do you want bold type, or did you not even notice it? Anti-references eliminate this guesswork.

### 3. Changing the Brief Mid-Project

A brief is a contract between you and the agency. When you change the target audience, add deliverables, or shift the brand direction after work has started, the earlier work becomes irrelevant. If the brief needs to change — and sometimes it legitimately does — communicate it explicitly and expect the timeline and cost to adjust.

### 4. Involving Too Many Stakeholders Too Late

The worst revision rounds happen when someone with authority sees the work for the first time at the final review. They have opinions that conflict with everything approved in earlier rounds. Get every decision-maker to sign off on the brief before work begins. If the CEO has veto power, the CEO needs to read the brief.

### 5. Skipping the Budget Conversation

Agencies are not trying to squeeze your maximum budget out of you. We are trying to figure out which tools and approaches fit your constraints. A project with a $3K budget gets a focused, efficient solution. A project with a $30K budget gets more exploration, more concepts, more refinement. Both are valid. But if we do not know which one we are working with, the first round will miss and we both lose time.

## What Happens After You Send the Brief

Once we receive your brief, here is what we do with it:

1. **Internal review.** The team reads the brief and flags questions or missing information. This takes one to two business days.
2. **Clarification call.** We schedule a 30-minute call to fill gaps, align on priorities, and confirm scope. This is not a sales call — it is a working session.
3. **Proposal.** We send a scoped proposal with deliverables, timeline, and cost. You approve, request changes, or decline. No pressure.
4. **Kickoff.** Once approved, we start with a kickoff meeting where we walk through the brief together with the full team.

The better your brief, the shorter steps one and two become. We have had projects go from brief to kickoff in 48 hours because the brief covered everything.
