01 — Overview

Outreach is a system, not a one-off message

  • Brand deals go to creators with sharp outreach skills, not the largest followings
  • Treating outreach as a repeatable system is what separates booked creators from idle ones
  • This guide covers the entire arc from preparation through closing deals

Brand deals do not arrive in your inbox because you exist on the internet. Creators with hundreds of thousands of followers sit idle, refreshing their DMs, while lesser-known creators with sharp outreach skills book three paid projects a month. The difference is never talent alone — it is whether you treat brand outreach as a repeatable system or leave it to chance.

At OKAD, we manage creator networks and produce UGC campaigns for brands across e-commerce, SaaS, food and beverage, and lifestyle verticals. We have seen what lands in marketing managers' inboxes — and what gets deleted in under two seconds. This guide distills everything we have learned into a step-by-step process you can start running today, whether you have fifty followers or fifty thousand.

What follows covers the entire arc: preparing before you send a single message, identifying the right person at each company, crafting emails and DMs that earn responses, building a follow-up cadence that converts silence into conversations, negotiating rates without leaving money behind, timing your outreach for maximum impact, and sidestepping the errors that sink most creators' pipelines.

  • Target: 15–20 personalized pitches per week
  • Channels: cold email (primary), DMs (bridge to email)
  • Follow-ups: 3-touch sequence, spaced 3–7 days apart
  • Templates: 6 email templates, 2 DM scripts, 3 follow-up messages
02 — Preparation

What to do before you contact anyone

  • Build a single, clean portfolio link with 4–6 of your strongest clips
  • Define your content positioning in one sentence before you write any outreach
  • If your portfolio is not ready, stop and build it first

Every brand contact you reach out to will do the same thing within ten seconds of opening your message: click whatever link you included and decide if you are credible. If that link leads to a disorganized Google Drive folder, a personal TikTok with no clear positioning, or worse, nothing at all — the conversation is over before it started.

The preparation checklist

  1. Build a single, clean portfolio link. Include four to six of your strongest UGC clips, a brief description of the niches and product categories you have created for (paid or unpaid work both count), a clear way to request your rates, and your contact information. A brand scanning your work does not want to scroll through forty random clips. They want to see four great ones that feel relevant to what they sell.
  2. Define your content positioning. Can you articulate in one sentence what kind of content you make, who it resonates with, and what makes your approach distinct? The creators who stand out are the ones who can say: "I produce unscripted product demos for wellness brands, filmed in natural light with a conversational tone that performs well in paid social." That specificity signals professionalism.
  3. Gather proof points. If previous content has driven measurable results — views, saves, click-through rates, conversion data — note those numbers. If you have worked with any recognizable brand, even on a small scale, make that visible. Social proof compresses the trust-building process from paragraphs to a single line.
  4. Set up a professional email address. Sending outreach from a Gmail account with your birth year in the handle undercuts everything else. A custom domain is ideal; a clean, name-based address is the minimum.

If your portfolio and positioning are not ready, stop here and build them first. Every template in this guide assumes you have a polished link to share.

03 — Contacts

How to reach the person who can say yes

  • Your pitch needs to reach someone with authority: marketing manager, partnerships lead, or founder
  • Check social bios, LinkedIn, team pages, and tagged collaborations
  • Reaching the right person is worth more than perfecting the wrong message

Sending a pitch to a generic "info@" or "hello@" address is the outreach equivalent of tossing a message in a bottle into the ocean. Your email needs to reach someone with the authority and context to act on it — typically a marketing manager, a brand partnerships lead, or, at smaller companies, the founder themselves.

Where to find contacts

  1. Start with social bios. Many DTC and e-commerce brands list a dedicated partnerships or press email directly in their Instagram or TikTok bio. This takes thirty seconds to check and is often the most direct route.
  2. Search LinkedIn strategically. Look up the company name combined with titles like "marketing manager," "influencer partnerships," "content lead," or "brand manager." At smaller companies with under fifty employees, one person typically manages the entire creator program.
  3. Check the company website. About pages, team pages, and career listings often reveal names and roles. Once you have a name, most companies follow a predictable email format: first@company.com, first.last@company.com, or firstinitial.last@company.com.
  4. Look at tagged collaborations. If another creator has posted sponsored content for the brand, check whether they tagged a specific person or thanked someone by name in comments. That person likely manages creator relationships.
  5. Use DMs only as a last resort. Direct messages on social platforms can work as an opening move, but they get buried in request folders and lack the formality needed for discussing budgets and contracts. When you do open via DM, move the conversation to email as quickly as possible.

Finding the right contact is the most labor-intensive part of outreach. At OKAD, when we run creator campaigns, this research phase is where most of the upfront time goes — because reaching the right person is worth more than perfecting the message sent to the wrong one.

04 — Email Templates

Cold emails that earn responses

  • Answer four questions in the first few lines: why this brand, what you create, what you propose, next step
  • Keep every message under 150 words
  • Six templates below cover every common scenario

A pitch that works answers four questions within the first few lines, without making the reader dig for the answers:

  • Why are you reaching out to this specific brand? Proof that the message is not copy-pasted.
  • What do you create? Your niche, format, and platform in one sentence.
  • What are you proposing? Paid content, affiliate arrangement, whitelisting rights — name the structure.
  • What is the next step? One simple action, not a multi-paragraph negotiation.

Every template below follows a five-part skeleton: a specific opener, a brief introduction of who you are, a statement of fit, a concrete offer, and a low-friction ask. Adapt the bracketed sections to each brand.

Template 1: The Standard Cold Pitch

Use this as your default when reaching out to a brand you have no prior relationship with.

Subject: Content concept for [Brand]

Hi [Name],

I came across [Brand] through [specific detail: a recent product drop, an ad I saw running on Meta, something genuine]. The way you position [product/category] stood out to me.

I produce short-form UGC in [your niche] — [one-line description of your style, e.g., "fast-paced product walkthroughs with a casual, unscripted feel"]. I think there is a natural overlap between what I create and what your audience responds to.

I would love to put together a paid content package for [Brand] — a few short videos designed for your paid ads or organic feed. My portfolio is here: [link].

Happy to share rates and turnaround details if this is of interest.

Best,
[Your Name]
[Portfolio link] | [Social handle]

Why this structure works: the opener proves relevance, the deliverable is named explicitly, and the call to action requires only a yes or no. There is nothing to decode.

Template 2: The Existing Customer Angle

This is the highest-converting pitch format — use it whenever you genuinely use the product.

Subject: Already using [Product] — content partnership idea

Hi [Name],

I have been a genuine [Product] customer for [timeframe], and I consistently find myself creating content around it organically. It felt like the right time to reach out and see if there is an opportunity to formalize that into a paid collaboration.

I create [niche] UGC — portfolio here: [link]. I would love to develop a small content package for [Brand] built around the same authentic angle I already take with your product, just with a proper brief and structure behind it.

Can I send over a few content concepts and my rates?

Thanks,
[Your Name]

Template 3: The Paid Media Pitch

Use this when you want to position your content specifically for the brand's advertising campaigns.

Subject: Ad creative for [Brand] — UGC creator

Hi [Name],

I noticed [Brand] is running creator-style ads across Meta and TikTok — that is exactly the format I specialize in. Portfolio here: [link].

I would like to propose a small batch of UGC videos engineered specifically for paid performance — hook-driven openings, product demos, or testimonial-style content, depending on what fits your current campaign goals.

Usage rights for paid social can be included in the package. Let me know if you would like a rate breakdown.

Best,
[Your Name]

Template 4: The Revenue-Share Proposal

Effective for brands that may not have large upfront budgets but offer ongoing earning potential.

Subject: Performance partnership — [Your Name] x [Brand]

Hi [Name],

I produce UGC in [niche] and believe there is genuine audience overlap between my content and [Brand]'s customer base. Instead of a flat-fee project, I would like to explore a commission-based or affiliate arrangement — I create content, drive tracked sales through a code or link, and we both benefit from the performance.

Portfolio: [link]

If you already run an affiliate program, I would love to be considered. If not, I am happy to discuss what that structure could look like.

Thanks,
[Your Name]

Former clients represent your warmest pipeline. Do not let them forget you.

Subject: Round two? New content concepts for [Brand]

Hi [Name],

Working together on [previous project] was genuinely one of my highlights this quarter. Since then, I have been experimenting with [new format/trend/angle], and I think it could translate really well for [Brand].

Best,

When a brand publicly announces they are seeking creators, use a focused, efficient response.

Hi [Name],

Saw you are looking for UGC creators for [campaign/program]. I create [niche] content and believe my approach is a strong match — portfolio and samples here: [link].

[Your Name]

05 — DM Scripts

When social messages are your only door

  • DMs are a bridge to email, not the negotiation venue
  • Never negotiate scope, rates, or deliverables inside a DM thread
  • Open the door, establish interest, transition to email

Direct messages should be treated as a bridge to email, not as the negotiation venue. DM threads get buried, lack threading, and feel informal in ways that make discussing budgets and contracts awkward. That said, some brands — particularly founder-led or very small companies — operate DM-first, and reaching them any other way is impractical.

Short DM for Instagram or TikTok

Hey [Name] — I create [niche] UGC content and think there could be a solid fit for paid content for [Brand]. Here is my portfolio: [link]. Happy to share more details here or over email if you have one. Let me know!

DM after engaging with their content

Hey! Been following your content for a while — your [specific post/campaign] really caught my attention. I produce similar style UGC and would love to create something for [Brand]. Portfolio: [link]. Is email the best way to discuss?

The rule: never negotiate scope, rates, or deliverables inside a DM thread. Use the message to open the door, establish interest, and transition to email where the real conversation can happen properly.

06 — Follow-Ups

Converting silence into conversations

  • Most positive responses come from follow-up 1 or 2, not the initial message
  • Three touches, spaced 3–7 days apart, is the right rhythm
  • The graceful close leaves the door open without burning goodwill

The majority of creators send a single outreach message, receive no reply, and conclude the brand is not interested. This is almost always wrong. Marketing teams are overwhelmed, inboxes are crowded, and your email likely arrived during a sprint, a product launch, or simply a busy Tuesday. Following up is not pushy — it is professional. What crosses the line is following up five times in four days.

A clean three-touch follow-up sequence, properly spaced, will recover a significant share of opportunities that would otherwise evaporate.

TouchTimingPurpose
Follow-Up 13–4 days after pitchGentle resurface — re-share portfolio
Follow-Up 2~1 week laterReduce barrier — offer to send rates directly
Follow-Up 3Final messageGraceful close — leave door open

Follow-Up 1: The Gentle Resurface

Send 3–4 days after the initial pitch.

Hi [Name],

Wanted to make sure my earlier message did not get lost — I still think there is a strong alignment between [Brand] and the type of content I create. Portfolio here if it helps: [link].

Happy to share rates whenever convenient.

Best,
[Your Name]

Follow-Up 2: Reduce the Barrier

Send about one week after Follow-Up 1.

Hi [Name],

One more quick note. If creator content is on your radar right now, I am happy to send rates directly — no call needed unless that is easier for you.

And if the timing is not right, completely understood. No hard feelings.

Best,
[Your Name]

Follow-Up 3: The Graceful Close

Final message in the sequence.

Hi [Name],

Wrapping up on this thread so I am not cluttering your inbox. If a UGC collaboration ever makes sense for [Brand] down the road, I would genuinely love to reconnect.

All the best,
[Your Name]

Three touches, each separated by several days, is the right rhythm. After the third, move on. The brands that respond will typically do so after touch one or two. The graceful close leaves the door open without burning any goodwill.

07 — Negotiation

How to talk money without underselling

  • Name your price first — never respond with "whatever works for you"
  • Price the content, not your audience size
  • Present packages, not unit prices

More creators lose income by quoting too low than by getting turned down. Setting and communicating your rates with confidence is a skill that compounds over your entire career. Here are the principles we consistently see successful creators follow:

  1. Never respond with "whatever works for you." This signals uncertainty about your own value and invites the brand to anchor at the lowest number they can justify. Name your price first.
  2. Price the content, not your audience size. Brands paying for UGC are buying production quality, strong hooks, and content that performs in paid media. Your follower count is irrelevant to that equation.
  3. Charge a premium for usage rights. A video the brand will run as a paid ad for three to six months delivers far more value than a single organic post. Your pricing should reflect that difference explicitly.
  4. Present packages, not unit prices. A bundle of three videos typically gets approved faster and pays more per unit than negotiating each video individually. Structure your rates in tiers.
  5. Establish a floor rate and do not breach it. You can always negotiate downward from your initial quote in response to scope adjustments. You can almost never negotiate upward once a number is on the table.

When a brand asks for your rates

Here is how I typically price content for a brand like [Brand]:

• 1 UGC video (organic use only): $[X]
• 1 UGC video + 90-day paid media usage rights: $[X]
• Package of 3 videos + usage: $[X]

Happy to adjust scope to fit your budget — let me know what range works and I can put together a tailored proposal.

If the brand offers product only

Appreciate the offer! I generally work on a paid basis for produced content, but I would be open to a hybrid model — product plus a flat fee for the deliverables. Would that be something worth exploring?

If the brand goes quiet after receiving your rates: send Follow-Up 2 from the sequence above. Silence after a rate request usually indicates a budget mismatch, not a lack of interest. Offering a smaller package at a lower total price often restarts the conversation.

09 — Subject Lines

Subject lines that get opened

  • Clarity beats cleverness every single time
  • The subject line has one job: earn the open
  • Short, specific, and professional

Your subject line is a filter. If it reads like spam, it gets treated like spam — either by the recipient's brain or their literal email filters.

Subject lines that performSubject lines to avoid
Content concept for [Brand]EXCITING OPPORTUNITY!!!
Paid partnership idea — [Your Name]Collab??
Already a customer of [Product] — quick ideaURGENT: partnership inquiry
Applying — UGC creator for [campaign name]Influencer proposal — please review

The subject line has one job: earn the open. It does not need to sell the partnership, explain your background, or convey enthusiasm. Short, specific, and professional.

10 — Mistakes

Errors that kill your response rate

  • Mass-blasting identical messages is the fastest way to get ignored
  • No portfolio link (or a broken one) kills more pitches than any other factor
  • Giving up after one email and going silent after rate objections

After reviewing thousands of creator pitches that flow through brand inboxes, these are the errors we see sabotaging otherwise capable creators:

  1. Sending identical messages to dozens of brands. Marketing managers can recognize a mass-blast instantly. One specific sentence about the brand is worth more than three paragraphs of generic flattery.
  2. Hiding the ask. If the reader has to guess what you want, they will not bother. State whether you are proposing paid content, affiliate work, whitelisting, or something else — explicitly and early.
  3. Including no portfolio link, or a broken one. This single issue kills more promising pitches than any other factor. Test your link before you send. Then test it again on mobile.
  4. Leading with follower metrics instead of content quality. UGC arrangements are content purchases, not reach deals. Frame your pitch around what you produce, not how many people watch your personal feed.
  5. Writing an essay when a paragraph would suffice. Your initial message should generate curiosity and establish credibility, not explain the entire scope of a potential relationship.
  6. Giving up after one unanswered email. Most positive responses come from the first or second follow-up, not the initial message. Build follow-ups into your system from the start.
  7. Going silent after a rate objection. A brief, professional counter-proposal — a smaller package, adjusted usage terms, or a pilot project — frequently saves deals that seemed dead.
  8. Negotiating full deals inside DM threads. Social media messages are for opening doors. Contracts, rates, and deliverable details belong in email where they can be referenced, forwarded, and tracked properly.

Pitching is a numbers game — until your reputation takes over

Even a beautifully crafted pitch will not convert every time. Expect single-digit reply rates on cold outreach when you are starting out, gradually improving as your portfolio strengthens and past brand names build social proof. This is precisely why consistency and volume beat a single "perfect" message: the creators landing the most deals are the ones running the most well-targeted outreach, not the ones spending an hour perfecting one email.

Over time, the equation shifts. Brands start coming to you. Past clients return for repeat projects. Referrals arrive. But that flywheel only starts spinning once you have done the manual work of proving the system, building the relationships, and establishing a track record that speaks for itself.

11 — FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Can I pitch brands if I have almost no followers?+
Absolutely. Most UGC deals are content purchases — brands are buying videos they can run as ads or post on their own channels, not paying for your personal reach. Your portfolio and the quality of your work matter far more than your follower count. Target brands that are specifically seeking UGC creators rather than influencer-style partnerships.
What are the essential components of a UGC pitch email?+
Five elements: a personalized opener that demonstrates you researched the brand, a single line about who you are and what you create, a clear statement of fit between your content and their product, a specific proposal (paid UGC, affiliate, ad creative), and one frictionless next step. The entire message should stay under 150 words.
How many pitches should I send each week?+
If you are serious about building a consistent deal flow, aim for fifteen to twenty targeted pitches per week. Cold outreach naturally has low response rates, so volume paired with genuine personalization — not spam — is what produces a reliable stream of opportunities.
What is the best way to find a brand contact's email?+
Check their social media bios for a partnerships email first. Then search LinkedIn for marketing or partnership roles at the company. Then look at the brand's website for team member names and deduce their email format. Direct messages are a last option, not a first choice.
Should I include pricing in my initial outreach?+
No. Offer to send rates once the brand expresses interest rather than leading with numbers. This keeps your first message focused on demonstrating fit, and it gives you room to tailor pricing after you understand what the brand actually needs.
How long should I wait between follow-ups?+
Three to four days after your initial pitch for the first follow-up, approximately one week after that for the second, and then a final closing message if you still have not heard back. Three total touchpoints is a professional and effective rhythm.
Is email or DM more effective for brand outreach?+
Email is almost always more effective for substantive outreach. It signals professionalism, supports proper threading, and is where budgets and contracts get discussed. Use DMs only when you cannot find an email, when the brand is clearly DM-first, or as a light initial touch before transitioning to email.
How do I handle a brand that offers only product, no payment?+
It is perfectly acceptable to decline or to counter-propose a hybrid arrangement: product plus a flat fee for the content. Be polite but clear about your professional rates. Some creators accept product-only deals early in their career to build portfolio examples, but this should be a strategic choice rather than a default.
